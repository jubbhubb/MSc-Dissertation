# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good Feedback for Students

## Overview

This thematic analysis examined student accounts of feedback across teaching, assessment, student representation, support services, accommodation, and wider university systems. Across the dataset, students defined “good feedback” not simply as the opportunity to complete a survey, but as a meaningful, safe, timely and reciprocal process in which their views are listened to, acted upon, and communicated back to them. The strongest message was that feedback loses value when it appears to disappear “into the void”; conversely, even small visible changes can significantly increase trust, motivation and engagement.

## 1. Actionability, Responsiveness and Closing the Loop

The most prominent theme was the need for feedback to produce visible outcomes. Students repeatedly distinguished between being asked for feedback and seeing anything happen afterwards. Comments such as “you’re filling it out, and then nothing happens” and “what’s the point of giving feedback and nobody’s acting on it?” illustrate a strong sense of futility when feedback is not followed by action.

Good feedback was associated with clear follow-up: staff summarising what students said, explaining what will change, and giving updates on progress. Positive examples included lecturers saying, “given the feedback that we’ve received… we decided to extend the deadline,” or reporting “these are the changes we’re making.” Students valued short-term and long-term action plans, particularly when changes occurred while they were still taking the course. Where immediate change was impossible, students still wanted acknowledgement, rationale and progress updates rather than silence.

## 2. Transparency, Accountability and Institutional Trust

Students wanted to know where feedback goes, who reads it, who has authority to act, and how decisions are made. A recurring concern was institutional opacity: “no one knows what happens to it afterwards,” “I don’t know how the hierarchy works here,” and “we know nothing at all.” This uncertainty weakened trust and contributed to perceptions of performative consultation.

Students also described being passed between departments, receiving unclear explanations, or being told issues were “not in our scope.” Good feedback therefore requires accountable ownership. Students suggested clearer escalation routes, meeting summaries, shared minutes, an ombudsman or external oversight, and named staff or committees responsible for follow-through. Transparency was also linked to trust in assessment and marking, especially where students received only numerical grades or could not access proof of grading.

## 3. Accessible and Inclusive Feedback Channels

Students valued multiple routes for giving feedback: surveys, forms, QR codes, email, discussion boards, class representatives, tutorials, focus groups, office hours, and open forums. No single method suited all students. Good feedback systems were described as multimodal, easy to find, and embedded into existing learning routines.

In-class feedback was convenient because students were already present, but it risked excluding absent students. Email reached many students but was often ignored or lost in communication overload. QR codes and simple online tools were seen as accessible, though students noted that scanning a code and forgetting the tab could prevent completion. Students also called for always-open feedback channels so they could raise issues “as they are happening in real time,” rather than waiting for formal surveys.

Accessibility also meant technical usability: mobile-friendly forms, text-to-speech compatibility, accessible formats, clear platforms, and language-sensitive design for international students.

## 4. Safety, Anonymity and Power-Sensitive Participation

Anonymity was repeatedly identified as essential for honest feedback, particularly when criticism concerned lecturers, tutors, marking, accommodation, or sensitive personal circumstances. Students feared judgement, retaliation, being “blacklisted,” or being treated differently by staff. Even when feedback was technically anonymous, students worried they could be identified through unique names, small classes, disability, nationality, or specific circumstances.

Power dynamics were especially salient where staff being criticised were present in meetings. Students described this as awkward, intimidating, or unsafe. Good feedback should therefore provide anonymous or confidential options, independent mediation, and routes where feedback about an individual does not go directly to that individual without safeguards. Students also recognised the need to balance anonymity with safeguarding, suggesting neutral administrators or external bodies could hold identifying information if necessary.

## 5. Dialogue, Relationships and Being Heard

Students strongly valued being listened to as people. Good feedback was relational, not merely administrative. Students contrasted impersonal forms with conversations where someone “actually cares,” listens, and responds. A simple acknowledgement could be meaningful: “sometimes all you want is at least an acknowledgement.”

Face-to-face and dialogic feedback were valued because they allow explanation, tone, empathy and clarification. Focus groups, tutorials, office hours and student-led discussions were seen as richer than one-way surveys. Students also valued staff who explicitly invited feedback, showed care, and treated comments respectfully. Conversely, defensive, dismissive, rude or mocking responses discouraged future participation.

Trusted relationships mattered. Personal tutors, approachable lecturers and regular tutorial contact enabled students to raise specific concerns. Where students had no relationship with staff, feedback felt riskier and less meaningful.

## 6. Timeliness, Continuity and Communication Flow

Timing was central. Students criticised end-of-course surveys because they mainly benefit future cohorts: “what about my cohort?” Good feedback should occur when students have enough experience to comment but there is still time to improve the course. Mid-semester feedback was valued when it enabled adjustment during the current learning period.

However, timing must avoid peak assessment periods, holidays, and weeks of heavy workload. Students noted that surveys often arrive alongside essays, exams or deadlines, making feedback a lower priority. They also wanted timely communication after submitting feedback: delays, ghosting, and unclear timelines caused frustration and uncertainty.

Continuity mattered too. Students wanted consistent systems across courses and regular opportunities from week one onward, rather than sporadic or late mechanisms.

## 7. Feedback Burden, Fatigue and Feasible Engagement

Students described substantial survey fatigue. Repetitive, generic, long or mandatory forms reduced motivation and encouraged superficial completion. Some students reported clicking through satisfaction ratings without thought after completing several similar surveys.

Good feedback processes should be low-burden but still meaningful. Students preferred short, targeted forms; progress bars; optional comments; rating scales with space for elaboration; and forms that can save progress. Feedback should not require students to “write an essay” unless they want to. Students also noted that feedback is often extra labour, particularly for those juggling coursework, jobs, commuting, caring responsibilities, disability or language barriers.

## 8. Representation, Collective Voice and Partnership

Student representatives were important intermediaries, but their effectiveness varied. Some students did not know who their representatives were, what they did, or how to contact them. Others saw representatives as useful bridges who could aggregate concerns and preserve anonymity.

Students valued collective feedback because shared concerns carry more weight. They wanted mechanisms for seeing whether others agreed, endorsing peer comments, and understanding the scale of issues. Focus groups and group discussions helped students feel less isolated and gave language to concerns they had not yet articulated.

However, students also warned that feedback often overrepresents engaged, confident, attending students while missing quieter, disengaged, marginalised or struggling students. Good systems must proactively reach underrepresented voices.

## 9. Educational Relevance: Assessment, Course and Learning Improvement

Students wanted feedback systems to address issues that directly affect learning: teaching quality, lecture style, course organisation, assessment design, workload, exam preparation, formative guidance and learning resources. Good assessment feedback was specific, explanatory and developmental, not simply marks or numbers.

Students criticised unclear coursework instructions, late assessment changes, misalignment between teaching and exams, insufficient sample papers, compressed teaching, excessive workload for credit value, and lack of formative feedback on outlines. They also valued feedback that identifies good practice, such as well-organised lecture notes or responsive teaching adjustments.

## 10. Student Context, Equity and Support Needs

Students emphasised that feedback cannot be separated from lived circumstances. Housing insecurity, commuting, transport costs, financial strain, childcare, disability, language barriers, international status, caring responsibilities and mental health all shaped whether students could engage with university processes.

Poor feedback and support systems disproportionately affected vulnerable students. Examples included inaccessible forms, unclear support pathways, lack of accommodation follow-up, financial hardship, and adjustments not being implemented. Good feedback must therefore be holistic, recognising that academic experience is connected to wellbeing and material conditions.

## 11. Institutional Systems, Resources and Structural Constraints

Many feedback problems were attributed not to individual staff but to wider structures: large cohorts, insufficient staffing, bureaucratic processes, disconnected departments, unclear platforms, inaccessible services, poor infrastructure, and campus separation. Students recognised that staff may want to help but lack power or resources.

Good feedback requires systems capable of acting on feedback. Students called for joined-up communication, adequate administrative capacity, clearer ownership, better resourcing, consistent platforms, and institutional learning from recurring issues.

## 12. Value, Impact and Student Motivation

Students were motivated to give feedback when they believed it mattered. Visible implementation increased willingness to participate; lack of change reduced it. Some students were altruistically motivated to help future cohorts, but many wanted current benefit, especially where programmes were short or fees were high.

Students linked feedback to value for money, particularly international fees. When support or educational provision felt poor, students questioned whether the university respected their investment. Good feedback therefore demonstrates that student time, money and experience are valued.

## 13. Belonging, Community and Campus Connection

Feedback was tied to belonging. Students who felt part of a community were more willing to contribute because changes might help peers. Conversely, students who felt the university was a “faceless institution” or merely a service provider felt less invested.

Satellite campuses, large cohorts, online learning, weak school identity and limited society links reduced belonging. Students wanted clearer connection to the wider university, stronger local community, and better integration across campuses and societies.

## 14. Clarity, Specificity and Constructive Guidance

Finally, good feedback requires clarity: clear purpose, clear questions, clear routes, clear criteria, and clear responses. Students disliked vague surveys and generic questions. They wanted feedback requests to explain why input was being sought, how it would be used, and what kind of response was helpful.

Specific, constructive feedback was seen as more actionable than broad dissatisfaction. Students also wanted the university’s responses to be specific: not “we will take this into account,” but what was heard, what will be done, what cannot be done, and why.

## Conclusion

Good feedback, from students’ perspective, is timely, safe, specific, inclusive, dialogic and visibly consequential. The University should move beyond feedback collection as a procedural exercise and strengthen feedback as an accountable relationship: students give time, trust and insight; the institution responds with acknowledgement, transparency, action and care.
