# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good Feedback for Students

## Introduction

This report presents a thematic analysis of coded interview data exploring students’ views on what makes feedback effective within the University. The dataset indicates that students understand “feedback” broadly: it includes feedback they receive on learning and assessment, opportunities to give feedback about courses and services, and the University’s response to that student voice. Across the data, good feedback was not seen as a single event, but as a trustworthy cycle: students need accessible opportunities to speak, timely responses, visible action, and clear communication about what has or has not changed.

Seven overarching themes were identified: **Goal-referenced**, **User-friendly**, **Timely**, **Tangible and transparent**, **Actionable**, **Ongoing**, and **Consistent**. Several additional issues—such as anonymity, representativeness, institutional trust, wellbeing, and belonging—cut across these themes and are incorporated throughout the report.

---

## 1. Goal-referenced feedback: relevant, specific and connected to learning

Students valued feedback processes that were clearly linked to the purpose of the course, assessment, or issue being discussed. Generic surveys and broad feedback forms were experienced as difficult to answer and less meaningful. Students preferred **specific, targeted prompts** that related directly to their course, learning experience, assessment design, workload, or observed problems.

A strong subtheme was that feedback should be **course-specific and context-sensitive**. Standardised questions may be useful for comparison, but students wanted space for questions tailored to the particular module, teaching format, assessment structure, or problems already known to the course team. When surveys did not ask about students’ “real problems”, students felt the process was disconnected from their lived experience.

Students also linked good feedback to **alignment with learning goals and expectations**. Concerns about workload, assessment weighting, course pace, exam difficulty, and unclear expectations before enrolment suggest that students want feedback mechanisms to identify whether learning activities and assessments genuinely support learning. In assessment contexts, good feedback should explain how marks relate to criteria and how students can improve.

Finally, students wanted the **purpose of feedback requests** to be explicit. They were more likely to engage when they understood why information was being collected, who would use it, and how it related to improving learning or student experience.

---

## 2. User-friendly feedback: accessible, low-burden and psychologically safe

A major theme was that feedback opportunities must be easy and safe to use. Students repeatedly identified long forms, repetitive surveys, mandatory open-text questions, and unclear time estimates as barriers. Shorter, targeted formats—such as Likert scales with optional comments, brief QR-code surveys, in-class paper forms, and simple end-of-tutorial feedback—were seen as more engaging.

However, low burden should not mean overly restrictive. Students valued ratings for ease, but also wanted optional spaces to explain issues in their own words. Closed questions alone were seen as limiting when students needed to describe complex or sensitive experiences.

Accessibility and inclusion were central. Students noted barriers linked to language, commuting, mobile access, email overload, unclear contact routes, and inaccessible or unreliable digital tools. Feedback opportunities need to be visible in places students already use, such as course pages, tutorials, and class communications, rather than relying solely on email. Multiple modes—online, paper, in-person, anonymous, representative, and conversational—were preferred because different students have different needs and comfort levels.

Psychological safety was particularly important. Students were more willing to provide honest feedback when anonymity was available, especially where criticism involved staff, assessment, placements, peer review, or sensitive personal circumstances. Fear of judgement, identification, retaliation, or negative consequences could suppress candour. At the same time, some students valued human contact and direct discussion. This suggests the need for **choice**: anonymous routes for safety, and personal routes for students who want dialogue and support.

User-friendly feedback was also relational. Students described face-to-face discussions, tutorials, small-group conversations, staff check-ins, and class representatives as lower-pressure ways to raise concerns. These formats can reduce isolation, normalise shared issues, and help quieter or underrepresented students contribute.

---

## 3. Timely feedback: early enough to matter

Timeliness was one of the strongest themes. Students repeatedly stated that end-of-semester or post-exam feedback is often too late to benefit the current cohort. Feedback gathered after a course has finished may help future students, but can feel useless or demotivating to those who raised the issue.

Students preferred feedback opportunities early enough for meaningful change within the current learning experience. Mid-semester feedback was often valued, but some students felt that even halfway through the semester could be too late if the course is short or if assessment deadlines are imminent. This indicates that timing should be matched to the rhythm of teaching and assessment, rather than fixed only by institutional calendars.

Rapid response mattered. Positive examples included quick polls leading to deadline extensions, immediate course changes, prompt lecturer responses, and office-hour conversations that resolved concerns while they were still actionable. Conversely, delayed committee processes, slow escalation, being passed between departments, and late responses from support services reduced the usefulness of feedback.

Students also wanted anticipatory feedback opportunities: routes to raise concerns before exams, before problems escalate, or when early signs of difficulty emerge. Good feedback systems therefore need both planned checkpoints and rapid-response mechanisms.

---

## 4. Tangible and transparent feedback: students need to see the pathway and outcome

Students’ trust depended heavily on transparency. Many codes described feedback as going into a “void”, with students unclear about who reads it, where it goes, who has authority to act, or whether it is used at all. Automated confirmations were not seen as sufficient. Students wanted acknowledgement that their feedback had been received and understood.

A key component of good feedback is **closing the loop**. Students valued response documents, summaries of feedback, shared minutes, class-level data, and lecturer responses to comments. They wanted to see “you said, we did” style reporting, but also more nuanced communication: what was raised, what will change, who is responsible, and by when.

Transparency was also important when change was not possible. Students did not always expect instant agreement or full implementation, but they wanted reasons, rationale and evidence. Vague statements such as “we will take this into account” were seen as unhelpful unless accompanied by detail. Listening and justification could be sufficient when the explanation was clear and respectful.

There was also a tension between transparency and confidentiality. Students wanted visible outcomes and collective summaries while preserving anonymity, especially for sensitive issues or small cohorts. Collated reporting, moderated responses, and careful handling of demographic or identifying data may help balance these needs.

Human dialogue also contributed to transparency. In-person responses made students feel heard because they could ask follow-up questions, clarify misunderstandings, and see that staff were engaging with their concerns.

---

## 5. Actionable feedback: linked to decisions, solutions and improvement

Students consistently defined effective feedback by whether it led to action. Feedback without implementation was described as disheartening, performative, or pointless. Visible change increased students’ motivation to participate in future feedback, while perceived inaction reduced trust and effort.

Good feedback requires **concrete action plans**. Students wanted specific next steps, timelines, responsible persons, and distinction between short-term and long-term changes. Where immediate change was not possible, acknowledgement and a clear plan still mattered. Partial solutions were valued when exact requests could not be met, provided the response was solution-focused rather than dismissive.

Students also wanted feedback to reach the right decision-makers. Concerns were raised about feedback channels lacking authority, student representatives not having clear implementation pathways, and issues being deflected between departments. Effective systems need appropriate routing, escalation routes, and institutional accountability for resolution.

The theme also applied to academic feedback. Students wanted more than marks or brief generic comments. Good assessment feedback should explain why marks were awarded, identify mistakes, clarify expectations, and provide practical guidance for improvement. Detailed comments, Q&A opportunities, earlier release of materials, and assessment clarification were valued because they supported learning action.

---

## 6. Ongoing feedback: a continuous cycle rather than a one-off survey

Students did not view feedback as a single survey event. They wanted a continuous cycle of raising issues, receiving responses, seeing action, and following up. Repeated unresolved issues damaged confidence, particularly when similar problems were raised by multiple cohorts without visible change.

Regular opportunities were preferred, including weekly or monthly check-ins, tutorial feedback cycles, open forums, course representative meetings, and always-available channels. These should not become excessive or burdensome, but students wanted more than one opportunity per semester, especially where issues could be fixed during teaching.

The role of student representatives was important but inconsistent. Reps were seen as useful intermediaries, particularly for anonymity and collective voice, but only if they had clear communication routes, regular meetings, and feedback returned to them and their peers. Without follow-up, representation could become another stalled pathway.

Students also recognised that some changes benefit future cohorts. However, current students were more motivated when they saw at least some immediate benefit, or when the University clearly explained how their input would improve future provision. Ongoing feedback culture depends on reciprocity: students contribute when they believe the institution listens, responds and learns.

---

## 7. Consistent feedback: reliable standards across courses and services

Students reported variation between courses, tutors, departments and services. Some courses were well-organised and responsive, while others lacked openness or follow-through. This inconsistency undermined trust because students’ experience of feedback depended too heavily on individual staff practice.

Students wanted more standardised feedback processes across courses, including shared platforms, clear response expectations, consistent anonymity options, and visible feedback loops. Standardisation should not remove course-specific tailoring, but should ensure all students receive a minimum quality of opportunity, acknowledgement and response.

Consistency was also relevant to assessment. Students raised concerns about unclear peer review consequences, perceived unfair marking, variable moderation, and differences in tutor feedback quality. Reliable criteria, moderation, and transparent marking processes were seen as necessary for fairness.

At institutional level, students reported conflicting or misleading information, unclear jurisdiction between complaints and appeals, inconsistent pre-enrolment information, and weak coordination between different parts of the University. Good feedback systems therefore require internal coordination as well as local responsiveness.

---

## Cross-cutting considerations

Several issues cut across all themes. First, **trust and voice efficacy** were central: students participate when they believe their voice can influence change. Second, **representativeness** matters: engaged students may be overrepresented, while struggling, quiet, commuting, international, disabled or isolated students may be missed. Third, students wanted feedback systems to include the broader student experience, including wellbeing, accessibility, financial hardship and belonging, rather than focusing only on academic content. Finally, respectful engagement was essential; dismissive responses could undermine the entire feedback culture.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Introduce a visible closed-loop feedback model** across courses: “you said, we did, we cannot do, next steps, responsible person, timeframe.”
2. **Collect feedback early and repeatedly**, with brief early checkpoints, mid-course review, and rapid-response routes for urgent issues.
3. **Use short, targeted and accessible formats**, with optional open-text responses and mobile-friendly design.
4. **Provide multiple feedback routes**, including anonymous forms, class reps, tutorials, office hours, focus groups and in-person discussion.
5. **Clarify feedback pathways**, including who reads feedback, who can act, and how issues are escalated.
6. **Ensure action plans accompany feedback summaries**, especially where change is delayed or not possible.
7. **Standardise minimum expectations** for feedback collection, acknowledgement, response and follow-up across departments.
8. **Protect psychological safety**, with optional anonymity, confidential reporting, and careful handling of sensitive feedback.
9. **Improve assessment feedback quality**, ensuring comments explain marks and support future improvement.
10. **Monitor representativeness**, using varied methods to include quieter, less engaged and underrepresented students.

---

## Conclusion

Students defined good feedback as relevant, accessible, timely, transparent, actionable, ongoing and consistent. The strongest message is that feedback must not disappear after collection. Students want to know that their views are heard, understood, routed to the right people, and acted upon where possible. Even when change cannot happen, clear explanation and respectful dialogue sustain trust. A high-quality feedback system should therefore operate as a visible relationship between students and the University: one based on psychological safety, responsiveness, accountability and continuous improvement.
