# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good University Feedback?

## Introduction

This report presents a thematic analysis of student perspectives on what makes feedback to the University effective, meaningful, and worth engaging with. Across the data, students described “good feedback” not simply as the opportunity to complete a survey, but as a visible, responsive, accessible, and ongoing process through which student experience can be improved. Students emphasised that feedback must be easy to give, clearly connected to course aims and student needs, acted upon in a timely way, and followed by transparent communication about what has changed or why change is not possible.

Seven overarching themes were identified: **User-friendly**, **Goal-referenced**, **Consistent**, **Timely**, **Ongoing**, **Actionable**, and **Tangible and transparent**. A further contextual theme, **Whole-student engagement**, captures issues that sit around feedback quality but strongly shape students’ willingness and ability to participate.

---

## Theme 1: User-friendly feedback processes

Students strongly emphasised that feedback mechanisms must be simple, accessible, and low burden. Many described current systems as too long, too repetitive, or too reliant on email. Email was seen as an ineffective channel because students receive large volumes of messages: “email’s not a good way of communicating with us… we get so many of them.” As a result, feedback requests may be missed or ignored.

A key feature of good feedback design was brevity. Students preferred short forms with clear progress indicators, simple rating scales, and optional comment boxes. They suggested formats such as “three or four yes or no questions. And then optional comments at the end.” Large open-text questions were experienced as demanding, particularly during busy academic periods, and could discourage completion.

Students also wanted **multiple routes** for giving feedback. Online forms, QR codes, paper forms, tutorial discussions, focus groups, and in-class activities were all seen as useful because different students prefer different modes. Embedding feedback opportunities within teaching sessions was viewed as particularly effective: students described lecturers distributing paper notes or QR codes at the end of lectures as easier and more likely to produce responses.

Psychological safety was central. Many students felt anonymity was necessary to give honest feedback without fear of judgement or damaging relationships with staff. As one student noted, “when it is anonymous, you can be as honest as possible.” However, some students also wanted the option to give named feedback where follow-up would be helpful. Good feedback systems therefore provide choice and control over identity.

Accessibility was also important. Students highlighted the needs of international students, non-native English speakers, disabled students, and those using mobile devices. Feedback forms need to be written in accessible language, culturally sensitive, smartphone-friendly, and compatible with assistive technologies. If forms are hard to complete, students may simply abandon them.

---

## Theme 2: Goal-referenced feedback

Students wanted feedback opportunities to be clearly connected to the specific goals, expectations, and realities of their courses. Generic surveys were often seen as less useful because they did not capture the particular issues affecting a module, assessment, or teaching format. Students argued that feedback prompts should be tailored to the course: “it would be much more constructive if the lecturer themselves designed the survey… it needs to be more relevant.”

Good feedback was also understood as a way of checking alignment between what a course promises and what students experience. This included workload, credit weighting, assessment demands, and advertised course aims. Students gave examples of courses where “the amount of work… doesn’t match 10 credits” or where important components were “not advertised” clearly enough.

Assessment expectations were a particular concern. Students described frustration when sample papers, guidance, or feedback did not adequately prepare them for the difficulty or format of exams. Clear success criteria, examples, and shared standards were seen as essential. Without these, students may not know what quality looks like or how to improve.

---

## Theme 3: Consistent feedback systems

Students reported considerable variation in feedback practice across courses. Some courses actively sought, acknowledged, and responded to feedback, while others appeared to do little with it. This inconsistency affected trust: students were unsure what to expect, where to submit concerns, or whether their input would matter.

A recurring recommendation was for a more standardised feedback system across the University. Students wanted consistency in how feedback is collected, how anonymity works, who receives it, and what response students can expect. One student suggested “a consistent system that was used for all courses,” while another noted that “there are too many different applications used.”

Students also wanted clearer accountability routes. Some described being passed between offices or staff members without anyone taking ownership of an issue. This was experienced as frustrating and inefficient: “it just takes ages to find out who you need to talk to.” Good feedback systems therefore require stable escalation pathways and clear responsibility for follow-up.

Some students also suggested independent or external review of feedback, particularly where staff may have a personal investment in a course. This was seen as a way to improve objectivity, fairness, and accountability.

---

## Theme 4: Timely feedback

Timing was one of the strongest themes. Students argued that feedback is only meaningful if it can improve the experience of the current cohort, not only future students. They were frustrated when feedback was collected too late to help them: “whatever feedback you’re giving will be applied to the next year’s class… how is it gonna do any good to me?”

Mid-semester feedback was sometimes viewed as too late, especially in short teaching blocks. By the time results are processed, students may have only a few weeks left. Students wanted earlier opportunities to raise issues “before it’s a problem,” particularly where problems could affect exams, learning gaps, or wellbeing.

Rapid in-course responsiveness was highly valued. Students gave positive examples where staff acted within hours, such as extending a deadline after polling students. These examples demonstrated that feedback can be powerful when it is collected and acted upon while still relevant.

However, students also noted that feedback requests often arrive during assessment-heavy periods. When surveys compete with deadlines, students are less likely to complete them. Good timing therefore requires sensitivity to workload, avoiding major deadline periods, and ensuring students have the capacity to engage thoughtfully.

Students also suggested that final course surveys should sometimes occur after marks and assignment feedback are returned, so they can comment on the full learning cycle, including assessment feedback.

---

## Theme 5: Ongoing feedback cycles

Students did not want feedback to be a one-off event. They preferred continuous or recurring opportunities to give feedback throughout a course, allowing problems to be identified, addressed, and reviewed. Suggestions included weekly or fortnightly check-ins, standing feedback forms, or always-open course feedback channels.

An always-open route was seen as valuable because students could report issues as they arise rather than waiting for a formal survey window. As one student explained, “as soon as you feel like something’s not right, you just put that down.”

Importantly, ongoing feedback must include iteration. Students wanted staff to return after changes had been made and ask whether the solution was working. This creates a cycle of feedback, action, and review. Without this, feedback can feel futile. Students described repeatedly raising the same issues in meetings or surveys without visible improvement, leading to disengagement: “I’m filling out the form with the same things every single time… it seems futile.”

---

## Theme 6: Actionable feedback

Students defined effective feedback as feedback that leads to concrete next steps. They were dissatisfied with vague responses such as “we will take this into account” or “this was discussed,” because these phrases do not explain what will actually change. Students wanted specific action plans that outline what will be done, by whom, and when.

Positive examples of actionable feedback included changing the day lecture videos were uploaded, adding Q&A sessions, extending deadlines, or adjusting teaching methods. These concrete changes helped students believe that feedback was worthwhile.

Students also valued feedback processes that prompt constructive suggestions, not only complaints. Forms should ask what is not working, why, and how it could be improved. This helps students provide more useful responses and supports staff in identifying feasible solutions.

The theme of actionability also applied to academic feedback on assignments. Students wanted comments that explain what was done well, what was wrong, and how to improve future work. Minimal comments or marks alone were not seen as developmental. Good feedback should therefore include “feed-forward” guidance that helps students improve subsequent performance.

Students also noted that feedback should preserve what is working well. Positive feedback is important because it helps staff identify effective practice that should be continued or shared across courses.

---

## Theme 7: Tangible and transparent feedback

Students repeatedly described poor feedback systems as feeling like “sending the form back into the void.” They often did not know who read their comments, how the information was processed, or what happened afterwards. This lack of visibility undermined trust and reduced motivation to participate.

Good feedback systems therefore need to make the process tangible. Students wanted acknowledgement that their feedback had been received, even where immediate change was not possible. They also wanted summaries of feedback results and clear explanations of actions taken. One valued format was a table showing the issue raised and the solution: “this is the question, this is answer; this is the issue, and this is our solution.”

Transparency also means explaining constraints. Students accepted that not all requests can be implemented, but they wanted honest reasons rather than vague refusals. As one student said, “Sometimes the answer is no, but tell us why.”

Some students also wanted collective visibility, where peers could see or endorse common concerns. This was seen as a way to show whether an issue is widespread and to reduce feelings of isolation: students could “look at how many of your peers agreed with you.” Such mechanisms may also help staff prioritise recurring concerns.

---

## Contextual theme: Whole-student engagement

Several issues did not fit neatly within feedback-quality themes but strongly influenced engagement. Students felt that feedback mechanisms often focus narrowly on academic delivery and do not adequately ask about wellbeing, workload, commuting, belonging, or wider campus experience. Yet these factors shape learning and participation.

Students also discussed incentives, such as vouchers or small credits, as motivators for participation. While incentives do not guarantee better feedback, they may help overcome low engagement, particularly when students feel busy or unconvinced that feedback will matter.

Finally, students linked feedback participation to belonging. If students experience University as a purely transactional relationship, they may be less motivated to contribute. Where they feel part of a community, feedback can be seen as a collective responsibility: even if change does not benefit them personally, it may benefit peers.

---

## Conclusion

Students define good University feedback as accessible, relevant, consistent, timely, ongoing, actionable, and transparent. The strongest message is that students need to see that feedback goes somewhere and leads to meaningful response. A good system should therefore:

- use short, accessible, multimodal formats;
- provide anonymous and named options;
- tailor questions to courses and assessments;
- collect feedback early enough to benefit current students;
- keep channels open throughout the course;
- provide clear action plans and explanations;
- report back on results and decisions;
- ensure consistent processes and accountability across courses.

Ultimately, good feedback is not a single survey but a relational process. Students are more willing to give thoughtful feedback when they trust that the University is listening, responding, and treating them as partners in improving education.
