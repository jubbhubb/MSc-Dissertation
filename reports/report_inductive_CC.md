# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good University Feedback Processes

## Introduction

This thematic analysis examined student views on what makes feedback to the University meaningful, trustworthy, and worthwhile. Across the data, students did not define “good feedback” simply as the opportunity to complete a survey or make a complaint. Rather, good feedback was understood as a relationship between students and the institution: students need accessible ways to speak, confidence that their views are safe to express, evidence that someone is listening, and visible action or explanation in response.

Seven overarching themes were identified: **Responsiveness, Accountability, and Closing the Feedback Loop; Inclusive Participation and Student Voice; Feedback Burden and Student Wellbeing; Quality and Design of Feedback Mechanisms; Clear, Timely, and Transparent Communication; Accessible and Navigable Feedback Systems; and Institutional and Staff Context.**

---

## 1. Responsiveness, Accountability, and Closing the Feedback Loop

The strongest theme was that feedback becomes meaningful only when students can see that it has been received, considered, and acted upon. Many students described current processes as futile: they put effort into providing feedback, but “then nothing happens,” leading them to question “why bother.” This sense of **feedback futility** undermined future engagement and reduced trust in university systems.

Students repeatedly emphasised the need to **close the feedback loop**. They wanted to know what feedback had been received, what would change, and why some changes were not possible. Importantly, students did not expect every request to be implemented. However, they wanted respectful explanations rather than silence or dismissal. As one student put it, “sometimes the answer is no, but tell us why.”

Timeliness was central to responsiveness. Students valued changes that benefited the current cohort, especially on short courses where delayed action only helps future students. While some students were motivated by improving things for those who come after them, many felt that feedback should also produce change “now,” where possible.

Students also identified an **accountability gap**. Feedback was often collected, but it was unclear who was responsible for responding, escalating, or ensuring resolution. Some suggested that feedback should be reviewed by someone beyond the individual course organiser, particularly when the feedback concerns that person’s teaching. Independent or external review was seen as a way to reduce conflicts of interest and increase accountability.

Overall, good feedback processes build trust. Students said that seeing feedback translated into action would make them much more willing to participate in future feedback activities.

---

## 2. Inclusive Participation and Student Voice

Students emphasised that good feedback systems must capture a broad and representative range of voices. Current mechanisms were seen as privileging students who are already engaged, confident, vocal, or “keen.” Conversely, students who are absent, struggling, overwhelmed, commuting, working, or disengaged may be least likely to respond, even though they may have the most important concerns. This creates a risk that the University is “asking the wrong people” about the issues.

Anonymity was described as essential for honest participation. Students wanted to be able to criticise teaching, assessment, services, or staff behaviour without fear of judgement, bias, or retaliation. This was particularly important where staff being criticised also mark work, provide opportunities, or hold authority over students. Partial anonymity was considered insufficient; for example, platforms that are anonymous to peers but not to instructors may still discourage candour.

At the same time, students wanted flexibility. Some preferred to attach their name when follow-up was useful, while others needed anonymity for sensitive issues. A good system would allow both.

Student representatives were viewed as important mediators, especially for collective or anonymous concerns. However, their effectiveness depended on visibility, training, communication channels, and whether the University acted on issues raised. Where feedback through representatives did not appear in minutes or lead to action, students experienced the system as ineffective.

Community also shaped participation. Students were more willing to provide feedback when they felt part of a collective, not merely individual consumers. Peer discussion helped students realise they were not alone and could transform private dissatisfaction into collective voice. Incentives, in-class feedback opportunities, and paper forms were also seen as useful ways to widen participation, although each method has limitations.

---

## 3. Feedback Burden and Student Wellbeing

Students reported significant **survey fatigue**. Many felt “bombarded” by repeated feedback requests across courses and university services. This overload led to boredom, disengagement, and lower-quality responses. Long forms were a particular barrier, especially when they contained many open-ended questions, did not show progress, or could not be saved and returned to later.

Feedback burden was closely connected to wellbeing. Students described time poverty caused by deadlines, jobs, commuting, caregiving, workload, and mental health pressures. In this context, repeated requests for feedback can feel like an additional demand rather than an opportunity to be heard.

Students also argued that feedback systems should not be limited to academic delivery. They wanted routes to raise concerns about wellbeing, workload, isolation, pastoral support, accommodation, transport, disability support, finance, and campus facilities. Some felt that when they raised mental health or workload concerns, staff redirected them to generic support resources rather than acknowledging that course design or workload could be part of the problem.

Good feedback systems therefore need to minimise unnecessary burden and recognise that student wellbeing is part of the learning environment. Proactive personal check-ins were suggested, particularly for students with low attendance, late submissions, or declining performance.

---

## 4. Quality and Design of Feedback Mechanisms

Students preferred feedback mechanisms that were short, simple, specific, and easy to complete. Examples included quick polls, rating scales, QR codes, short in-class questions, or simple “smiley/sad face” responses. These mechanisms were seen as more likely to generate high response rates than long generic surveys.

Generic questions were criticised as vague and repetitive. Students wanted questions tailored to the course, week, assessment, or issue under review. They found it easier to provide useful feedback when the University asked about specific aspects of their experience rather than broad questions such as “what could we do better?”

Students also distinguished constructive feedback from venting. Effective systems should help students explain what the problem is, why it matters, and what improvement they would suggest. This does not mean excluding negative feedback, but structuring opportunities so that criticism can be translated into actionable information.

A further insight was reciprocity: students may provide more thoughtful feedback when they themselves receive detailed, respectful academic feedback from staff. Good feedback cultures appear to be relational; when students feel staff take care in giving feedback to them, they are more likely to give careful feedback in return.

---

## 5. Clear, Timely, and Transparent Communication

Communication was central to students’ understanding of good feedback. Many did not know who reads survey responses, how feedback is analysed, where it goes, or what happens after submission. Students wanted transparency about process: who receives feedback, who has authority to act, what stage a response has reached, and how decisions are made.

Timing was also important. Feedback requests often arrived during deadlines, exams, or late in the semester, when students had little time or when change was no longer possible. Students recommended earlier and ongoing opportunities, including feedback around a quarter of the way through the semester and continuously open suggestion channels.

Students also wanted aggregate results to be shared publicly and anonymously. Seeing collective feedback would help students understand whether others share their concerns and would increase accountability. Some suggested systems where students could see, support, or “like” issues raised by others, helping staff identify how widespread a concern is.

Email communication was a barrier. Students described receiving too many emails, causing feedback requests to be missed or ignored. Good communication should therefore use multiple routes and avoid relying solely on email.

---

## 6. Accessible and Navigable Feedback Systems

Students wanted multiple routes for feedback because no single method suits everyone. Suggested channels included online forms, paper forms, tutorials, in-person discussions, student representatives, anonymous suggestion boxes, QR codes, and standardised digital platforms. Tutorials were seen as useful because they are smaller and less intimidating, particularly where tutors are not the main lecturer.

However, students also wanted consistency. Multiple platforms across courses created confusion. A standardised feedback platform, with clear anonymity settings and regular acknowledgement, could make the system easier to navigate.

Navigation problems extended beyond course feedback. Students described being passed between departments, services, and offices without resolution, particularly for disability, finance, accommodation, appeals, and administrative issues. Many did not know “how the hierarchy works” or how to contact the University beyond their course.

Accessibility barriers were particularly important for disabled students, international students, non-native English speakers, commuters, and students based away from central campus. Long English-language emails, email-based systems, inaccessible support processes, and in-person meetings at inconvenient locations all reduced participation. Students also raised repeated unresolved issues around transport and infrastructure, especially for geographically isolated campuses.

Good feedback systems must therefore be easy to find, easy to use, accessible in different formats, and connected to clear routes for escalation and resolution.

---

## 7. Institutional and Staff Context

Students’ willingness to give feedback was strongly shaped by their experience of the institution and staff culture. Some described the University as a large, faceless structure where students are “talking to a wall.” Institutional scale was recognised as a genuine challenge, but it was also experienced as distance, slowness, and lack of dialogue.

Staff attitude mattered greatly. Students distinguished staff who actively invite, acknowledge, and respond to feedback from those who are defensive, dismissive, or aggressive. When staff appeared to care, students found it easier to engage. Conversely, defensive responses discouraged future feedback and made students feel that negative views were unwelcome.

Course size also shaped experiences. In large classes, students felt less heard and less personally connected, while smaller classes enabled more direct discussion and quicker responses. Students also used feedback to raise concerns about unclear teaching, poor organisation, assessment difficulty, and mismatch between credits and workload. In some cases, students felt staff relied on formal workload calculations that did not reflect lived experience.

Students recognised staff workload, strikes, and wider pressures, and some worried that giving feedback would create more work for already pressured staff. However, this recognition did not remove their need for response, teaching quality, support, and accountability.

---

## Conclusion

For students, good feedback is not simply a mechanism for collecting views. It is a visible, responsive, and trustworthy process through which students can influence their educational and wider university experience. The core finding is that students are more willing to provide feedback when they believe it is safe, worthwhile, accessible, and likely to receive a response.

Key implications for the University are:

- Close the feedback loop consistently: acknowledge, respond, act, and explain.
- Reduce survey fatigue by using shorter, better-timed, more targeted feedback tools.
- Provide multiple feedback routes, including anonymous and named options.
- Improve transparency about who receives feedback and what happens next.
- Make feedback systems accessible to students with different needs, locations, languages, and time pressures.
- Strengthen student representative systems and independent escalation routes.
- Treat wellbeing, workload, services, and infrastructure as legitimate feedback issues, not only academic delivery.
- Support a staff culture that welcomes feedback without defensiveness.

Ultimately, students want evidence that their voice matters. When feedback leads to dialogue, explanation, and visible change, it builds trust and encourages continued participation.
