# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good Feedback to Students

## Introduction

This report summarises key themes identified from qualitative student data about what makes University feedback processes effective. Across accounts, students described “good feedback” not simply as the opportunity to submit comments, but as a meaningful cycle in which their views are invited, heard, acted upon, and communicated back to them. Students’ experiences suggest that feedback quality depends on both process and outcome: how easy it is to give feedback, whether it feels safe and relevant, whether staff respond transparently, and whether students can see change.

Seven overarching themes were identified: **Tangible and transparent**, **Timely**, **Ongoing**, **Actionable**, **Goal-referenced**, **Consistent**, and **User-friendly**. Additional contextual issues concerned wellbeing, belonging, and motivation to participate.

---

## 1. Tangible and Transparent Feedback

A dominant theme was that feedback must feel visible and consequential. Students repeatedly contrasted effective feedback with experiences where comments seemed to disappear “into the void.” Many did not know who read their feedback, where it went, or whether it had any influence. This lack of transparency undermined trust and reduced motivation to engage in future feedback exercises.

Students strongly valued **acknowledgement**. Even where change was not immediately possible, they wanted confirmation that their feedback had been received and considered. As one student put it, “Sometimes all you want is at least… an acknowledgement.” For students, acknowledgement functioned as a minimum standard of respect and responsiveness.

Transparency was also important in relation to **ownership and process**. Students wanted to know who was responsible for reviewing feedback, what stages it passed through, and who had authority to act. This was particularly important for systemic issues, such as transport or facilities, where students were often told that “discussions” were happening but were not informed about progress.

Students also valued feedback summaries and clear reporting of outcomes. Examples of good practice included tables summarising issues raised and responses such as: “this is the issue, and this is our solution.” Importantly, students did not expect every request to be accepted. However, when change was not possible, they wanted a clear rationale. Responses such as “that’s how we do it” were viewed as inadequate. Transparency therefore means both showing what will change and explaining what cannot change, and why.

A related subtheme was **collective visibility**. Some students suggested mechanisms where peers could see or endorse issues, helping staff understand whether a concern was isolated or widespread and helping students feel less alone in raising concerns.

---

## 2. Timely Feedback

Students emphasised that feedback must be collected and acted upon while it is still relevant. A common frustration was that feedback appeared to benefit future cohorts rather than the students who gave it. Students questioned the value of feedback if “whatever feedback you’re giving will be applied to the next year’s class” and not to their own learning experience.

Timing was especially important in short semesters. Students felt that mid-semester feedback could already be too late if results were analysed and returned only when the course was nearly finished. They wanted opportunities to raise concerns before problems affected learning, assessment preparation, or exam performance. This was expressed as a desire for feedback “before it’s a problem.”

Students gave positive examples where rapid responses made feedback feel effective. In one case, students were polled about a deadline and received an extension within hours. Such examples were powerful because they showed responsiveness while the issue still mattered.

Timing also needs to consider the whole learning cycle. Some students suggested that final course surveys should be completed after receiving marks and assessment feedback, because assignment feedback is a major part of the course experience. However, requests for feedback should also avoid assessment-heavy periods, when students are less likely to respond due to deadlines.

Overall, timely feedback is not simply “early” feedback. It requires matching feedback opportunities to the rhythms of the course: early enough to address problems, rapid enough to remain useful, and placed at points where students can evaluate important aspects of their learning.

---

## 3. Ongoing Feedback

Students preferred feedback as a continuous process rather than a one-off survey. They described value in regular opportunities to comment throughout a course, such as weekly or fortnightly check-ins, standing feedback forms, or repeated short activities. This would allow issues to be identified as they emerge rather than waiting for formal survey windows.

Ongoing feedback was also linked to **iteration**. Students wanted to see changes implemented, then be asked whether those changes were working. For example, they suggested an overview of feedback followed by a later update showing “what changes have been made,” and then further consultation about whether the response had helped.

In contrast, repeated feedback without visible improvement was experienced as futile. Students described raising the same concerns in multiple staff-student liaison committee meetings or repeatedly writing the same points on forms, only to receive minimal response. This repetition discouraged engagement and contributed to cynicism.

Good feedback systems therefore need to be cyclical: collect feedback, respond, implement or explain, review effectiveness, and continue the conversation.

---

## 4. Actionable Feedback

Students defined effective feedback as feedback that leads to specific, observable action. They were critical of vague institutional language such as “we will take this into account” or “this was discussed,” because such phrases do not specify what will happen. Students wanted concrete next steps, named actions, and implementation plans.

Good examples included specific changes such as uploading lecture videos earlier, providing additional Q&A sessions, changing teaching approaches, or extending deadlines. These examples were viewed positively because the link between student feedback and staff action was visible.

The theme of actionability also applied to **assessment feedback**. Students wanted comments that helped them improve future work, not just marks or brief statements. Effective assessment feedback identified what was done well, what was wrong, and how to improve. In this sense, good feedback is also “feed-forward”: it supports students’ future learning rather than only evaluating past performance.

Students also suggested that feedback prompts should be designed to generate actionable responses. Rather than asking only what students liked or disliked, forms should ask why an issue exists and how it might be improved. This encourages constructive suggestions and makes responses easier to translate into change.

Actionability also includes recognising and preserving what works well. Students noted that feedback should not only collect complaints, but should identify effective teaching practices so they can be continued or shared across courses.

---

## 5. Goal-Referenced Feedback

Students wanted feedback processes to be meaningfully connected to the aims, standards, and expectations of specific courses. Generic survey questions were often seen as less useful because they did not reflect the distinctive structure, assessments, or problems of a particular module. Students suggested that lecturers or course teams should design or supplement surveys with course-specific questions.

Goal-referenced feedback also involved checking whether the course matched what had been advertised. Students raised concerns about workload, credit weighting, and assessment components that felt misaligned with expectations. For example, they described cases where the amount of work did not match the credit value, or where a major course component had not been clearly communicated in advance.

Assessment expectations were a further issue. Students wanted clearer standards, more examples, and better alignment between teaching, sample materials, and exams. Where only one sample paper was provided and the actual exam was much harder, students felt poorly prepared. Good feedback processes should therefore help identify gaps between stated learning outcomes, teaching practices, assessment expectations, and student experience.

In this theme, feedback is effective when it is anchored to clear goals: what the course promised, what students were expected to learn, what standards they were being assessed against, and whether the learning experience supported those goals.

---

## 6. Consistent Feedback Systems

Students described substantial variation across courses in how feedback was requested, acknowledged, and acted upon. Some courses provided strong feedback loops, while others offered little visibility or response. This inconsistency created uncertainty and reduced confidence in the University’s feedback culture.

Students recommended a more standardised system across courses. They wanted to know where to give feedback, how anonymity works, when feedback will be reviewed, and what type of response they can expect. Standardisation was also seen as important because students currently encounter too many different platforms and approaches.

Consistency does not mean every course must use identical questions. Rather, students appeared to want consistent expectations and infrastructure, alongside course-specific content. A common framework could ensure that all students receive acknowledgement, summary reporting, and clear action planning, while allowing local adaptation.

Some students also wanted independent oversight. They suggested that feedback might be reviewed by someone outside the course to reduce conflicts of interest and increase objectivity. This was particularly relevant where feedback concerned teaching quality or where students feared staff might be defensive.

Students also described being passed between offices when trying to raise issues, leading to frustration and delay. A good system needs stable accountability routes, so students know who owns an issue and how it will be followed up.

---

## 7. User-Friendly Feedback

Students were clear that feedback systems must be easy, accessible, and safe to use. Survey fatigue was a major concern. Students felt overwhelmed by repeated requests across multiple courses, especially when forms were long, repetitive, or arrived during busy periods. This reduced both completion rates and quality of responses.

Students preferred shorter forms with progress indicators, quick rating scales, and optional comment boxes. They valued flexibility: some students want to provide detailed comments, while others can only manage brief responses. Large open-ended questions were often experienced as burdensome.

Communication channels also mattered. Email alone was viewed as ineffective because students receive too many messages and may miss survey links. Students suggested multiple modes, including in-class paper forms, QR codes, tutorials, online forms, focus groups, and class discussions. Embedding feedback into teaching time was seen as especially effective because it reduced effort and improved participation.

Psychological safety was central. Many students felt anonymity was necessary for honest feedback, particularly when comments might be critical or when relationships with staff could be affected. However, some also wanted named feedback routes where personal follow-up was useful. The preferred approach was therefore to offer both anonymous and identifiable options.

Inclusive design was also important. International students and non-native English speakers may need clearer language and culturally sensitive routes. Digital accessibility was also raised, including mobile-friendly forms and compatibility with assistive technologies. If forms are difficult to complete on phones or inaccessible, students may abandon them.

Small group formats, such as tutorials or listening groups, were often seen as safer and more personal than large lectures or impersonal surveys. These settings helped students feel that others shared their concerns and that their views were being heard in context.

---

## Additional Contextual Themes

Some issues extended beyond the main feedback-quality themes but are important for understanding engagement. Students felt that feedback mechanisms often focus narrowly on academic delivery and do not always capture wellbeing, workload, commuting, belonging, or campus experience. These factors shape learning and may need dedicated feedback routes.

Motivation was also discussed. Incentives such as vouchers or small rewards may increase participation, but students’ deeper motivation appeared linked to whether they felt part of a community. When students experience the University as a shared community, they may give feedback not only for themselves but to improve conditions for peers and future students.

---

## Conclusion and Implications

Students define good feedback as a visible, timely, ongoing, actionable, goal-referenced, consistent, and user-friendly process. The central message is that feedback should not end when a student submits a form. It should form a transparent cycle in which students know their comments were received, understand who is responsible, see what action is taken, and receive explanations where change is not possible.

Key implications for the University are:

- Provide routine acknowledgement and “you said, we did/we cannot because…” responses.
- Collect feedback early and repeatedly enough to benefit the current cohort.
- Create standing channels for emerging issues.
- Require concrete action plans rather than vague responses.
- Use course-specific questions aligned with learning aims and assessment expectations.
- Standardise core feedback processes while allowing local adaptation.
- Reduce burden through short, accessible, multimodal feedback formats.
- Offer both anonymous and named routes.
- Ensure feedback includes academic, practical, and wellbeing-related aspects of student experience.

Overall, students are willing to engage when feedback feels meaningful. Trust is built when the University demonstrates that student voice has a clear destination, a fair hearing, and a visible impact.
