# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good Feedback for Students

## Overview

The analysis identified several interrelated qualities that students associate with “good feedback” from the University. Although participants often spoke about feedback in broad terms—including academic feedback, course evaluation, student representation, administrative responses, and support services—the same core principles recurred across contexts. Good feedback was understood not simply as information delivered to students, but as part of a visible, responsive relationship between students and the institution.

Procedural material, participant background, and out-of-scope comments were excluded from theme development. The main overarching themes were: **tangible and transparent**, **actionable**, **timely**, **ongoing**, **user-friendly**, **consistent**, and **goal-referenced** feedback.

---

## Theme 1: Tangible and Transparent Feedback

Students repeatedly emphasised that feedback needs to be visible, understandable, and traceable. A major frustration was that feedback often felt as though it disappeared into a “void”, with students unclear about who received it, whether it was read, or what happened next. One participant summarised this clearly: *“we might share feedback, and then we don’t know what happens with that… even if they make changes… we might not see it.”*

Transparency was important at multiple stages: knowing where feedback goes, who is responsible for acting on it, what decisions have been made, and why some changes cannot happen. Students were more accepting of negative or limited outcomes when the rationale was explained. As one participant noted, *“sometimes we ask, and the answer is no, but tell us why… ‘just because it’s how we do it’ isn’t an answer.”*

Positive examples involved staff summarising what students had said and reporting back on planned changes: *“they gave a summary of the good points… the bad points… here’s what we’re going to do in the short term, here’s what we’re going to do in the long term.”* Such reporting made students feel heard and increased trust in the process.

Transparency also applied to academic assessment. Students valued explanatory comments showing why marks were awarded, such as: *“she wrote a comment for each question and said, ‘why you got this point’.”* By contrast, marks without explanation, or feedback consisting only of numbers, were perceived as insufficient.

---

## Theme 2: Actionable Feedback

Students viewed feedback as effective when it led to concrete next steps. Good feedback did not merely acknowledge a problem; it explained what would happen, who would do it, and how it would help. Students were critical of responses such as *“we’ll take this into account”* when no clear action followed. One participant said this phrase was frustrating because *“what does it mean? like they won’t do anything.”*

Actionable feedback included specific teaching adjustments, support strategies, or course changes. Examples included extending deadlines after a poll showed students were struggling, changing upload days for lecture videos, adding Q&A sessions, and providing additional resources. Students valued these changes because they could see a direct connection between feedback and outcome: *“many people are struggling with the course… and then the lecturer… extended the deadline.”*

In academic work, actionable feedback meant more than identifying mistakes. Students wanted guidance for future improvement: what was strong, what was weak, and what should be done differently next time. Very brief feedback, such as *“two word feedback”* or *“a sentence per question”*, was seen as inadequate, especially for substantial assignments.

Participants also highlighted the importance of feedback being directed to those with power to act. Feedback was less useful when responsibility was passed around or when students were told to contact another office without guidance. Good feedback systems therefore require clear ownership and escalation routes.

---

## Theme 3: Timely Feedback

Timing was one of the strongest themes. Feedback was considered most useful when provided early enough for students to act on it or benefit from resulting changes. End-of-semester feedback was often seen as too late: *“a lot of the forms come when the semester is already ending. so that won’t help us to solve the ongoing problem with the course.”*

Students valued mid-course feedback when it allowed adjustments during the same semester: *“when you’ve had enough experience… but then there’s still time to fix it.”* However, some participants felt that even mid-semester could be too late in short courses, suggesting earlier points such as week three or four.

Timeliness was especially important around assessment. Students wanted feedback before exams or deadlines when it could still shape preparation. Post-exam feedback was useful for reflection, but insufficient if the problem had already affected performance: *“whatever damage was done last semester is done for the exam.”*

Prompt responses to queries also mattered. Delayed or absent replies to emails, appeals, marking queries, disability support, or administrative problems were experienced as harmful because opportunities could be lost. One student described a response arriving after they had already solved the problem themselves: *“by the time I had already fixed it… this was no longer helpful.”*

---

## Theme 4: Feedback as an Ongoing Process

Students did not want feedback to be a one-off event. They described good feedback as an iterative cycle: students raise concerns, staff respond, changes are made, and students are later told whether those changes worked. This was often described as “closing the feedback loop”.

Repeated failure to close the loop led to disengagement: *“you never get any kind of response to your feedback… you don’t want to give feedback anymore.”* Conversely, seeing feedback acted upon motivated future engagement: *“if they did implement some change… I’d be more willing to do the surveys.”*

Students valued regular, embedded feedback opportunities, such as weekly or monthly check-ins, class representative meetings, town halls, personal tutor contact, and open forums. Ongoing channels were particularly important because issues arise throughout a course, not only at formal evaluation points. One participant suggested *“a space for feedback that’s present from week one to the end of the course.”*

The ongoing theme also included current-cohort benefit. Students recognised that feedback may improve future cohorts’ experiences, but repeatedly questioned why current students should not also benefit: *“whatever feedback you’re giving will be applied to next year’s class… how is it gonna do any good to me?”*

---

## Theme 5: User-Friendly Feedback

Students described feedback processes as more effective when they were easy, accessible, safe, and respectful. Long, repetitive forms reduced engagement: *“if it’s too long, then I do not want to… I’m more likely to just lose interest.”* Students preferred concise formats, progress indicators, simple rating options with optional text boxes, and opportunities to save progress.

Accessibility was a major concern. Mobile-unfriendly forms, excessive email traffic, fragmented platforms, and unclear routes discouraged participation. Students wanted feedback channels placed where they already work, such as course pages, lectures, tutorials, or commonly used platforms.

Students also valued multiple routes: online, in-person, anonymous, representative-mediated, and direct conversation. Different students preferred different formats depending on confidence, language, disability, sensitivity of the issue, and relationship with staff. One participant summarised this as needing *“different avenues… whichever option suits them.”*

Psychological safety was important. Some students feared being identifiable, judged, or treated differently if they gave critical feedback. Smaller tutorials, trusted intermediaries, student-led groups, and anonymous channels were seen as safer. Feedback was also more usable when communication was respectful; dismissive or rude responses made students less willing to engage.

Language and cultural accessibility were also raised. International students and non-native English speakers described needing clearer language, support with technical vocabulary, and culturally sensitive approaches. Good feedback was therefore not only available but understandable.

---

## Theme 6: Consistent Feedback

Students experienced feedback quality as variable across courses, staff, schools, and platforms. Some courses provided clear responses and visible change, while others offered little acknowledgement. This inconsistency reduced trust: *“sometimes, yes, sometimes, no, it depends on the context.”*

Consistency was important in assessment feedback, marking, communication, and support. Students were concerned when marking processes seemed opaque, when different tutors gave different levels of guidance, or when adjustments depended on individual staff willingness. One participant noted that tutor quality *“varies,”* while another described poor lecturer-tutor communication leading tutors to be unsure what they were doing.

Students wanted more standardised feedback systems, platforms, and expectations across courses. Standardisation did not mean eliminating local flexibility, but ensuring that all students receive a reliable minimum level of feedback, response, and follow-up.

Consistency also involved fairness and objectivity. Where feedback concerned staff behaviour or course-level problems, participants suggested moderation, independent oversight, or escalation routes so responses did not depend solely on the individual being criticised.

---

## Theme 7: Goal-Referenced Feedback

Good feedback was also expected to relate clearly to learning goals, assessment standards, and student priorities. Students valued feedback that helped them understand what successful performance looks like and how their work measured against expectations.

Negative examples included unclear coursework instructions, sample papers that did not reflect exam difficulty, or assessments that students felt were not aligned with taught content. One participant said students wanted *“a better idea of this exam,”* while another noted that an exam did not make them feel like better or worse practitioners because the connection to competence was unclear.

Goal-referenced feedback also meant asking the right questions. Students criticised generic or “copy-pasted” surveys that did not capture course-specific problems. More useful feedback prompts were specific, contextualised, and connected to real issues, such as a particular lecture, topic, assessment, or learning activity.

Some students also wanted feedback systems to address broader student experience goals, including wellbeing, workload, accessibility, and campus life, rather than only module content.

---

## Conclusion and Implications

Across the data, students defined good feedback as **clear, specific, responsive, timely, accessible, and connected to change**. The most damaging feedback experiences were those where students invested effort but received no acknowledgement, no explanation, and no visible outcome. The most positive examples involved staff explicitly saying what had been heard, what would change, what could not change, and why.

Key recommendations are:

1. **Close the feedback loop routinely** by reporting what was heard, what will change, and what cannot change.
2. **Act quickly where possible**, especially during short courses and before assessments.
3. **Provide concrete academic guidance**, not marks or vague comments alone.
4. **Use accessible, low-burden, multi-channel formats** with options for anonymity and dialogue.
5. **Create consistent minimum standards** for feedback quality, response times, and follow-up across courses.
6. **Ensure feedback reaches decision-makers** and that responsibility for action is clear.
7. **Make feedback goal-referenced**, tied to assessment criteria, course aims, and students’ real concerns.

Overall, good feedback is not experienced as a single message or form. It is experienced as an ongoing relationship in which students can see that the University listens, responds, explains, and improves.
