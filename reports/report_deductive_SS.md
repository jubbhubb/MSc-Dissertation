# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good Feedback from the University?

## Introduction

This thematic analysis examined student accounts of feedback across academic, course-level and wider institutional contexts. Although some interview material concerned broader student voice, representation, accommodation, finance, or research logistics, the analysis below focuses on the overarching themes most relevant to what students understand as “good feedback” from the University. Across the dataset, students described good feedback not simply as information given after assessment, but as a wider communicative process: feedback should be clear, timely, useful, responsive, accessible, fair, and visibly connected to change.

Seven overarching themes were identified: **Tangible and Transparent**, **Ongoing**, **Actionable**, **Timely**, **User-Friendly**, **Consistent**, and **Goal-Referenced**.

---

## Theme 1: Tangible and Transparent Feedback

The strongest theme across the data was the need for feedback to be visible, understandable, and accountable. Students frequently described existing feedback processes as opaque: they did not know who read their comments, where feedback went, whether it was acted on, or how decisions were made. This lack of transparency made feedback feel like “talking to the void” or “talking to a wall.”

Good feedback was therefore understood as feedback that clearly shows students: **what has been heard, who is responsible, what decision has been made, and why**. Students valued examples where staff summarised feedback, identified key issues, and explained what would happen next. One student praised a course where staff “send us back something” and said, “these are the things we’re gonna do now.” Another valued communications that set out “this is the question, this is answer; this is the issue, and this is our solution.”

Transparency also applied to assessment feedback. Students criticised marks without explanation, minimal comments, unclear marking criteria, and anonymous marking processes that prevented follow-up. Numerical marks alone were seen as insufficient: “exam feedback… would be just a number.” In contrast, feedback was valued when it explained why marks were awarded, gave comments per question, or clarified how performance related to criteria.

Students also wanted transparent rationales when change was not possible. A refusal was more acceptable if accompanied by a meaningful explanation: “sometimes we ask, and the answer is no, but tell us why.”

---

## Theme 2: Feedback as an Ongoing Process

Students repeatedly framed good feedback as a **cycle**, not a one-off event. The phrase “closing the feedback loop” appeared across the data and captured a central concern: feedback should be received, considered, responded to, acted upon where possible, and revisited.

A common frustration was that students were repeatedly asked for feedback but rarely heard anything back. This reduced motivation to participate: “you never get any kind of response to your feedback… you don’t want to give feedback anymore.” Students described repeated concerns being raised across semesters or years without visible change, which undermined trust.

Good practice examples involved repeated opportunities for dialogue: weekly or monthly feedback mechanisms, regular course representative meetings, office hours, personal tutor check-ins, and mid-course discussions. Students valued courses where staff routinely asked for feedback and returned to students with updates. Ongoing feedback was also important because it allowed students to benefit while still enrolled, rather than only improving the experience for future cohorts.

Students recognised that some changes take time, but wanted evidence of progress. Even where change was slow, acknowledgement and updates sustained engagement. Feedback was most meaningful when it was part of an iterative process: try something, ask whether it worked, adapt again.

---

## Theme 3: Actionable Feedback

Students consistently defined effective feedback by whether it enabled improvement. Feedback should not merely acknowledge a problem; it should provide concrete next steps, practical solutions, or clear routes for action.

In academic work, actionable feedback meant more than “good” or “bad.” Students wanted specific examples, explanation of strengths and weaknesses, and guidance on how to improve: “give specific examples… be more detailed about what was right, what was wrong.” Minimal feedback, such as “two word feedback” or “eight sentences” on a major project, was experienced as inadequate because it did not help students improve.

At course and institutional level, actionable feedback meant translating student concerns into visible changes. Positive examples included extending deadlines after a poll showed many students were struggling, changing when lecture videos were uploaded, adding Q&A sessions, altering practical sessions, or introducing new support resources. Students valued when feedback produced “action items” rather than vague assurances.

Conversely, responses such as “we’ll take this into account” or “we can’t do anything” were criticised as non-actionable unless accompanied by a plan, explanation, or alternative route. Students wanted staff to say: what will be done, who will do it, when it will happen, and what students should do next.

---

## Theme 4: Timely Feedback

Timeliness was central to whether feedback was useful. Students repeatedly stated that feedback given too late loses value, particularly when it arrives after an assessment, after a course has ended, or after the current cohort can no longer benefit.

Students wanted feedback while there was still time to act. Mid-semester feedback was viewed positively when it enabled in-course change, but several students felt even mid-semester could be too late in short courses. Some suggested feedback in week three or four, or multiple points across the semester. One student summarised effective timing as: “when you’ve had enough experience… but then there’s still time to fix it.”

Students also emphasised anticipatory feedback before high-stakes assessment. They wanted clearer guidance before exams about expectations, sample difficulty, and whether they were adequately prepared. Feedback after an exam was seen as too late if it only confirmed problems students had already experienced.

Timeliness also applied to administrative communication. Delayed responses about timetables, appeals, finance, disability support, or course access had serious consequences, including missed opportunities and increased stress. Students were clear that delayed feedback or advice becomes “no longer helpful.”

---

## Theme 5: User-Friendly Feedback

Good feedback processes were described as accessible, manageable, inclusive, and emotionally safe. Students criticised long, repetitive surveys, forms that were not mobile-friendly, excessive email requests, unclear platforms, and processes requiring students to chase multiple people. Survey fatigue was a major barrier: repeated generic forms made students disengage or provide rushed responses.

Students preferred feedback systems that were simple, short, and embedded into existing teaching moments. Examples included QR codes in lectures, short polls, paper forms in tutorials, quick rating scales with optional comment boxes, and always-open course feedback forms. Students wanted a balance between low-effort formats and opportunities for detail.

User-friendly feedback also required flexible modes. Some students preferred online anonymity; others valued face-to-face discussion. Many argued that both should be available. Smaller tutorials, office hours, and personal conversations were seen as more relational and less intimidating than large meetings or impersonal forms.

Accessibility was especially important for disabled students, international students, non-native English speakers, commuters, and students with caring responsibilities. Students highlighted barriers created by email-only communication, inaccessible formatting, technical language, and lack of reasonable adjustments. Good feedback should use clear, student-centred language and be available through multiple routes.

Psychological safety was also crucial. Students described discomfort when giving critical feedback directly to lecturers, especially where power dynamics or fear of consequences were present. Respectful, non-judgemental communication was therefore central to user-friendly feedback.

---

## Theme 6: Consistent Feedback

Students valued reliability and fairness across courses, tutors, markers, and services. Many accounts described feedback quality varying substantially depending on the individual tutor, lecturer, course organiser, or school. Some students received detailed, helpful comments in one course but minimal or unclear feedback in another.

Consistency was important in assessment. Students raised concerns about inconsistent marking, unclear moderation, unreliable peer assessment, variation between tutorial groups, and unstable assessment guidance. Good feedback should be based on shared criteria, applied fairly, and supported by moderation where necessary.

Students also wanted consistency in feedback systems. Different courses using different platforms, formats, or expectations made it difficult to know where to find feedback or how to raise concerns. Several students suggested a standardised feedback platform or clearer university-wide expectations, while still allowing course-specific questions.

Consistency did not mean ignoring context. Students recognised that different courses have different needs, but wanted stable principles: feedback should be accessible, explained, timely, and followed up everywhere.

---

## Theme 7: Goal-Referenced Feedback

Students wanted feedback to be clearly linked to goals, criteria, expectations, and meaningful student priorities. In assessment, this meant alignment between teaching, learning outcomes, sample materials, and exams. Students criticised exams that did not reflect taught content or sample papers, and feedback that did not clarify how performance related to intended competence.

Goal-referenced feedback also meant asking the right questions. Students criticised generic “satisfied/dissatisfied” surveys and copy-pasted forms that did not address course-specific issues or wider concerns such as wellbeing, workload, facilities, or support. Feedback requests felt more meaningful when tailored to the course, a recent change, or a specific problem.

Students also wanted feedback to reflect their actual starting points and needs. For example, international students described needing language and vocabulary support; direct-entry students needed preparatory resources earlier; students in large or diverse cohorts needed feedback systems that recognised different levels of prior knowledge.

Good feedback therefore helps students understand: **What am I aiming for? Where am I now? What needs to change? How can I improve?**

---

## Conclusion

Students’ accounts show that good feedback is not only about the quality of written comments on assignments. It is a broader relationship between students and the University. Good feedback is transparent, timely, actionable, ongoing, accessible, consistent, and linked to clear goals.

The most important finding is that students want the University to **close the feedback loop**. They are more willing to engage when they see that feedback is read, understood, responded to, and used. Even where change is not possible, students value acknowledgement, explanation, and evidence that their views have been seriously considered.

In practical terms, the University should prioritise: clearer communication about who receives feedback; routine reporting back on feedback outcomes; earlier and more frequent feedback points; concise and accessible formats; specific, course-relevant prompts; clear action plans; and consistent standards across schools and courses. These changes would help students experience feedback as meaningful, trustworthy, and genuinely developmental.
