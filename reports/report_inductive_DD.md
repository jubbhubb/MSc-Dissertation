# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good University Feedback?

## Introduction

This thematic analysis synthesises student accounts of feedback processes across teaching, support services, representation systems and wider University life. Across the dataset, students understood “good feedback” not simply as the opportunity to submit comments, but as a reciprocal process: students give feedback, the University listens, responds, explains, and acts. Effective feedback was therefore judged by its psychological safety, accessibility, relevance, timeliness, and visible impact.

Eleven overarching themes were identified.

---

## 1. Timely, visible action and closing the feedback loop

The strongest theme was that feedback is only meaningful when students can see what happens afterwards. Many students described current processes as sending feedback “into the void”, “talking to a wall”, or completing surveys without knowing whether anyone had read them. Acknowledgement was seen as the minimum acceptable response: students wanted confirmation that feedback had been received, understood and passed to the right people.

However, acknowledgement alone was not enough. Students wanted specific communication about: what feedback was raised; what will change; what cannot change; why; and when updates will follow. Vague phrases such as “we will take this into account” were experienced as frustrating unless linked to concrete action.

Timeliness was central. Mid-course or early-course feedback was valued because it could improve the experience of the current cohort. End-of-course feedback was often seen as benefiting only future students, which was particularly demotivating for one-year master’s students or students on short, intensive courses. Students recognised that not every issue can be solved immediately, but wanted evidence of effort, short-term fixes where possible, and honest explanations where change would take longer.

Good practice examples included courses where staff summarised midterm feedback, identified action points, and implemented changes within the same semester. These experiences increased trust and motivation to give future feedback.

---

## 2. Psychological safety, anonymity and trust

Students repeatedly emphasised that honest feedback requires safety. Many worried that critical comments could affect how lecturers, tutors, markers or accommodation staff perceived them. This was especially important where the person receiving feedback also held power over assessment, progression, references or living arrangements.

Anonymity was therefore viewed as essential for candid feedback, particularly when comments concerned teaching quality, staff behaviour, discrimination, disability, mental health, accommodation conflict, or personal circumstances. Even when systems were described as anonymous, some students remained anxious that they could be identified through unique names, writing style, small cohorts or specific situations.

There was also a tension between transparency and confidentiality. Students wanted to know who reads feedback and where it goes, but feared that if feedback went directly to lecturers, honesty might reduce. They therefore suggested independent moderation, collated reporting, or external oversight as ways to protect students while still ensuring feedback reaches decision-makers.

Trust was damaged by dismissive, defensive or hostile staff responses. Conversely, trust increased when staff responded respectfully, acknowledged criticism, and showed willingness to adapt.

---

## 3. Participation burden, motivation and fatigue

Students described feedback as labour requiring time, effort and emotional energy. Long surveys, repeated requests, poorly timed emails and excessive open-text questions contributed to fatigue. Many students reported being “bombarded” with surveys and selectively completing only those that felt relevant or likely to matter.

Motivation depended strongly on perceived impact. Students were more willing to complete feedback when they wanted a specific change, believed staff would listen, or had previously seen feedback acted on. Where repeated feedback produced no change, students questioned “what’s the point?” and disengaged.

Convenience mattered. Students preferred short, low-effort mechanisms, progress bars, optional comment boxes, save-progress functions, QR codes, in-class time, or quick rating tools. Incentives such as vouchers, food, extra credit or access to useful resources were also seen as increasing participation, though students still wanted the process to be meaningful rather than transactional.

Emotional labour was particularly evident for student representatives, who had to translate peer frustration into constructive comments without seeming too critical of staff.

---

## 4. Feedback quality, specificity and design

Students wanted feedback tools that were concise but expressive. Pure Likert-scale surveys were quick and accessible, but often too limited to explain why students were satisfied or dissatisfied. Conversely, large open-text boxes could feel overwhelming. The preferred design was a structured survey with ratings, specific prompts, and optional spaces for explanation.

Generic, copy-pasted surveys were criticised for failing to capture course-specific issues. Students wanted questions tailored to actual course design, assessment, workload, teaching methods and known concerns. They also wanted prompts that encouraged constructive responses, such as “what should change?”, “why?”, and “what worked well?”

Students noted that feedback should be specific enough to be actionable, but not so narrow that it misses broader structural problems. Some concerns, such as workload-credit mismatch, large cohorts, assessment bunching, or poor course organisation, require escalation beyond individual lecturers.

Students also connected feedback quality to assessment feedback. Poor academic feedback—such as marks without explanation or generic comments—limited students’ ability to improve. Good feedback should therefore apply both to institutional listening and to the feedback students receive on their work.

---

## 5. Clear communication channels and navigable feedback routes

Students often did not know who to contact, where feedback went, or which office was responsible for particular issues. They described being passed between departments, receiving contradictory advice, or escalating simple questions to senior staff because normal routes were unclear.

Good feedback requires clear pathways. Students wanted a central hub or staffed contact point that could route issues correctly, explain responsibilities, and prevent students from chasing multiple disconnected offices. They also wanted clearer distinction between course-level, programme-level, school-level and University-wide issues.

Multiple channels were preferred: anonymous surveys, class reps, office hours, tutorials, online suggestion boxes, QR codes, paper forms, focus groups and direct email. However, students stressed that channels must be easy to find and clearly explained. Email alone was often ineffective due to overload, formality and intimidation.

---

## 6. Accessible and inclusive feedback opportunities

Students highlighted that feedback systems can exclude those with disabilities, caring responsibilities, commuting demands, language barriers, financial pressures, low confidence, or limited time. Digital systems were convenient for some but inaccessible for others, especially when forms were not mobile-friendly or were buried in email.

In-person feedback could increase response rates, but excluded absent students, commuters, distant-campus students and those unable to attend. Therefore, accessibility requires multimodal options.

Students wanted feedback to account for diverse circumstances, including international students, mature students, student parents, disabled students, low-income students, and students on distant campuses. Accessibility should be built in by default rather than treated as an individual adjustment. Feedback questions should also address lived student problems such as childcare, housing, finance, transport, wellbeing and disability support—not only teaching satisfaction.

---

## 7. Student voice, representation and partnership

Student representatives were viewed as important intermediaries, especially for students who felt uncomfortable approaching staff directly. However, their role was often unclear, poorly publicised, or limited by lack of authority. Some students did not know who their representatives were or how to contact them.

Representatives could also feel burdened, dismissed or blamed when presenting collective feedback. Students argued that responsibility for institutional change should not sit with unpaid student representatives alone.

Good practice would involve clearer rep visibility, earlier appointment, dedicated online spaces, stronger communication channels, and regular meetings with accountable staff. Students also supported more participatory approaches, including student-designed surveys, open forums, listening groups for underrepresented students, and peer-visible platforms where students can endorse shared concerns.

Representativeness was a concern. Feedback often captures vocal, engaged or present students, while quieter, overwhelmed, absent or disengaged students may be missing. Good systems need active outreach to these groups.

---

## 8. Dialogic, relational and caring feedback culture

Students valued feedback most when it felt like dialogue rather than data extraction. Face-to-face conversations, focus groups, tutorial discussions, personal tutor meetings and regular check-ins were described as more meaningful because they allowed tone, context and emotion to be communicated.

Students wanted staff to explicitly invite feedback, show openness, respond non-defensively, and treat students as whole people. Feeling cared for increased willingness to speak. Personal tutors, supportive lecturers and proactive staff outreach were especially valued when they helped students disclose difficulties and access support.

However, dialogue must be balanced with anonymity. Some students wanted direct conversation; others needed protected channels. Good feedback culture therefore requires both relational contact and safe anonymous routes.

---

## 9. Institutional systems, barriers and responsibility boundaries

Students frequently framed the University as large, complex and impersonal. Bureaucracy, unclear hierarchies and fragmented departments undermined confidence that feedback would lead to change. Students described problems being deflected between services, with no clear accountability.

They also recognised that individual lecturers may care but lack power over structural issues such as cohort size, platforms, timetabling, transport, accommodation, staffing or University policy. Good feedback therefore requires clarity about who controls what, and escalation routes when local staff cannot act.

Students wanted the University to avoid using external responsibility as deflection. Even when issues involve third parties, such as transport providers, students expected the University to advocate, negotiate and communicate progress.

---

## 10. Learning, teaching and support improvement

Feedback was understood as a route to improving education and support. Students raised issues including teaching quality, assessment design, workload, course organisation, feedback on assignments, classroom capacity, study space, transport, accommodation, disability support and wellbeing.

A key finding is that students do not separate academic experience from wider life conditions. Workload, mental health, commuting, financial pressure, childcare, accommodation and campus facilities all affect learning. Feedback systems focused only on course content miss important determinants of student success.

Students wanted staff to recognise that wellbeing and workload are educational issues, not separate concerns outside course responsibility.

---

## 11. Belonging, community and peer connection

Belonging shaped feedback engagement. Students who felt part of a community were more motivated to improve it for themselves and others. Conversely, students who experienced the University as transactional or impersonal were less likely to invest effort in feedback.

Peer spaces helped students validate concerns and realise they were not alone. Societies, group chats and informal discussions often felt more responsive than formal structures, although unmoderated peer spaces could become negative or unconstructive.

Distant-campus students particularly described feeling disconnected from the wider University. For them, feedback about transport, campus culture, representation and access to central services was also feedback about belonging.

---

## Conclusion

Students define good feedback as safe, accessible, specific, timely, dialogic and consequential. The central message is that asking for feedback is not enough. Students need to know that their input is heard, protected, routed appropriately, responded to transparently, and acted on where possible.

The University should prioritise:

1. Clear feedback-loop closure after every major feedback exercise.  
2. Earlier and ongoing opportunities for current-cohort change.  
3. Anonymous and protected routes alongside relational dialogue.  
4. Short, tailored, mobile-accessible feedback tools with optional detail.  
5. Clear routing, ownership and escalation pathways.  
6. Stronger support for representatives without overburdening them.  
7. Inclusive outreach to underrepresented and less engaged students.  
8. A broader view of feedback that includes wellbeing, support, facilities, finance, transport and belonging.

Good feedback is ultimately experienced by students as evidence that the University respects them, cares about their experience, and is willing to change.
