# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good Feedback at the University

## Overview

This thematic analysis synthesises student accounts of feedback within the University, including feedback students give to courses, departments, services and the wider institution, as well as feedback and responses they receive in return. Across the data, “good feedback” was not understood simply as a form, survey, or assessment comment. Rather, students described effective feedback as a relational and institutional process: it should be easy to give, safe to express, clearly listened to, acted upon where possible, and followed up transparently.

A central finding is that students are often willing to contribute feedback, but motivation declines when feedback appears to disappear “into the void”, when surveys are repetitive or burdensome, or when institutional responses are delayed, vague, defensive, or absent. Conversely, students are encouraged when staff acknowledge concerns, explain constraints, make visible changes, and treat students as partners in improving teaching and the wider student experience.

---

## Theme 1: Feedback as Dialogue, Student Voice and Partnership

Students wanted feedback to be a two-way process rather than a one-directional act of filling in forms. Good feedback was associated with being able to “actually talk” to lecturers, tutors, representatives, or decision-makers, especially where issues were complex or emotionally charged. Focus groups, tutorials, staff-student liaison meetings, listening groups, and course representative systems were all valued when they enabled discussion rather than simple data extraction.

Students repeatedly emphasised wanting their “voices heard”. However, being asked for feedback was not enough: student voice needed to be taken seriously and translated into action. One student described feedback as meaningful when staff reported, “most people voted for this. so we’re gonna go over this again.” This type of response helped students feel that their input mattered.

Student representatives were often positioned as useful intermediaries, particularly where students felt uncomfortable approaching lecturers directly. Reps could aggregate common concerns, provide a semi-anonymous channel, and bring issues to formal meetings. However, the representative system was inconsistent. Some students were unsure who their reps were, how to contact them, or whether feedback passed through reps reached the right people. Where representatives lacked visibility, support, authority, or communication channels, the role risked appearing tokenistic.

Students also valued peer-to-peer feedback spaces. Informal group chats, societies, peer discussions and listening groups helped students realise that concerns were shared rather than individual. This collective validation could make students more confident to raise concerns formally.

---

## Theme 2: Timeliness, Responsiveness and Closing the Feedback Loop

The strongest and most recurrent theme was the need to close the feedback loop. Students wanted acknowledgement of feedback, explanation of what had been heard, clarity on what would happen next, and later updates on implementation. Without this, feedback felt futile. One student explained that after putting effort into feedback, “nothing happens… a lot of the time, it feels very futile.” Another described forms as “sending the form back into the void”.

Timing was central. End-of-semester feedback was often perceived as too late to help current students: “a lot of the forms come when the semester is already ending. so that won’t help us to solve the ongoing problem.” Students preferred feedback points early enough for change to occur during the course, such as weeks two to four, mid-course check-ins, or short weekly/fortnightly opportunities. Feedback was most valued when it enabled immediate or near-immediate action, such as deadline extensions, additional Q&A sessions, changes to teaching materials, or revisiting difficult content.

Students distinguished between short-term and long-term responses. They accepted that some issues require time, but wanted institutions to separate what can be done now from what will inform future cohorts. Good practice involved telling students: these are the short-term changes, these are the longer-term priorities, and this is why some requests cannot be implemented.

Acknowledgement alone was helpful but insufficient if repeated concerns were not addressed. Students were frustrated when feedback was repeatedly “heard” but not acted upon, or when responses were limited to “that’s how we do it.” A good response should include rationale, action, or a constructive explanation of constraints.

---

## Theme 3: Institutional Accountability, Transparency and Coordinated Systems

Students often described the University as large, complex, and difficult to navigate. Feedback could feel lost in an “enormous system”, with uncertainty about who reads it, where it goes, and who has the authority to act. Students wanted clearer feedback pathways: who receives feedback, who is responsible, how it is processed, and how decisions are made.

Transparency was particularly important where change was not possible. Students were more accepting of constraints when these were explained honestly. Silence, vague responses, or appeals to tradition undermined trust. Students criticised responses such as “this is how we’ve always done it” because they did not demonstrate reflection or accountability.

A recurring problem was fragmentation. Students described being passed between departments, emails circulating without resolution, unclear complaint routes, inconsistent digital platforms, and support services redirecting rather than resolving issues. These experiences reduced trust and increased the burden on students to chase answers.

Students also wanted feedback to reach the “right people”: course organisers for course-specific issues, teaching staff for delivery issues, school leadership for structural issues, and senior university decision-makers for cross-institutional concerns. Some suggested independent or external oversight to reduce defensiveness and ensure accountability, especially when feedback concerns staff behaviour, course design, or repeated unresolved problems.

---

## Theme 4: Accessible, Low-Burden and Multimodal Feedback Routes

Good feedback mechanisms were described as easy, visible, and low effort. Students preferred concise forms, progress indicators, optional comments, simple rating scales, QR codes, embedded course-page links, and short in-class opportunities. Long forms, mandatory questions, repeated open-text boxes, and unclear completion times discouraged participation. One student summarised the problem: “why do i have to fill like nine different questions when i only want to give one point across?”

Survey fatigue was widespread. Students reported being “bombarded” with surveys, receiving repetitive requests, and ignoring emails because feedback felt excessive or low-impact. Feedback requests competed with coursework, employment, commuting, caring responsibilities and fatigue. Low-burden design was therefore not just convenience but an inclusion issue.

Students did not want one single mode only. Multimodality was repeatedly endorsed: online forms, in-person paper slips, tutorials, focus groups, anonymous suggestion boxes, Piazza/Top Hat polls, class reps, direct emails, and office hours each served different needs. In-class paper feedback was valued because it created immediate opportunity, while online routes supported absent, commuting, or anxious students. Students wanted choice.

Accessibility also included platform consistency. Too many digital systems across courses made feedback harder to find and use. A consistent, visible feedback hub or standardised feedback location within course pages was suggested.

---

## Theme 5: Psychological Safety, Trust and Respectful Handling of Feedback

Students’ willingness to provide honest feedback depended heavily on safety. Anonymity was repeatedly described as essential, especially for criticism of lecturers, sensitive personal issues, disability-related needs, or complaints about treatment. Students worried that feedback could be traced through email, small classes, unique identities, or platform settings where posts were anonymous to peers but visible to organisers.

Power dynamics were a major barrier. Students feared being judged, treated differently, or harmed academically if they criticised staff. Some described being uncomfortable giving feedback when the staff member concerned was present. Others referred to fear of retaliation, defensiveness, ridicule, or being labelled difficult.

Good feedback systems therefore require more than a statement of anonymity. Students wanted clarity about who can identify them, who sees raw comments, whether comments are moderated, and how confidentiality is protected. Some suggested external administrators or neutral moderators to preserve accountability while reducing fear.

Respectful response mattered. Dismissive, defensive, blaming or humiliating staff responses damaged trust and discouraged future feedback. Conversely, non-judgemental, empathetic and constructive responses made students more willing to engage.

---

## Theme 6: Clear, Specific, Constructive and Actionable Feedback Content

Students distinguished between vague feedback and useful feedback. Generic satisfaction questions were often seen as insufficient. Course-specific and issue-specific prompts helped students give more meaningful responses, particularly when linked to lectures, labs, assessments, workload, teaching style, resources, or specific points of difficulty.

Students valued prompts that asked not only “what did you dislike?” but “why?” and “how could it be improved?” This supported constructive rather than purely complaint-based feedback. However, students also warned that requiring extensive written explanations for every question increased fatigue. The preferred model was often structured questions plus optional elaboration.

Good institutional responses also needed specificity. Students wanted summaries of what was said, what will change, what will not change, and why. A particularly positive example involved a course team producing a table of feedback by section, including positive and negative comments and planned actions. This made feedback feel organised, fair, and consequential.

---

## Theme 7: Inclusive, Context-Sensitive and Holistic Feedback

Students’ feedback needs varied by programme, year, campus, identity, workload, disability, language, commuting status, caring responsibilities, and whether they were undergraduate, postgraduate, international, mature, or student parents. Standardised feedback mechanisms risked privileging already engaged students while missing those who were struggling, absent, overworked, isolated, or less confident.

Students noted that in-class feedback overrepresents attendees, while email surveys may be ignored. Students who most need support may be least likely to respond. Feedback systems therefore need proactive inclusion strategies, such as targeted listening groups, accessible formats, translation or language-conscious design, support for commuting and satellite-campus students, and mechanisms for underrepresented groups to be heard.

Students also argued that feedback should address the whole student experience, not only course content. Workload, mental health, accommodation, transport, finances, disability support, campus belonging and administrative systems all shaped learning. Feedback that only asks “are lectures good?” misses key conditions affecting engagement and performance.

---

## Theme 8: Staff Capability, Teaching Quality and Local Implementation

Students recognised that good feedback often depends on local staff culture. Some lecturers were seen as responsive, caring, and willing to adapt; others were perceived as dismissive, defensive, unavailable, or constrained by systems. Staff openness strongly influenced whether students felt feedback was worthwhile.

Teaching quality and assessment were major feedback domains. Students raised concerns about compressed course structures, workload-credit mismatch, unclear assessment expectations, insufficient exam preparation, group work fairness, and inconsistent quality of assessment feedback. Good feedback from the University should therefore include actionable academic guidance, clear marking rationales, timely formative support, and alignment between teaching and assessment.

Students also noted that subject expertise does not automatically equal teaching effectiveness. Feedback systems should support staff development, not simply collect student dissatisfaction.

---

## Conclusion and Implications

Students described good feedback as: **anonymous where needed, multimodal, timely, specific, transparent, relational, and visibly acted upon**. The most damaging pattern was repeated collection without visible response. The most encouraging pattern was acknowledgement plus action, even small action.

Key priorities for the University are:

1. **Close the feedback loop consistently**: report what was heard, what will change, what cannot change, and why.  
2. **Collect feedback early and often enough to benefit current students**, not only future cohorts.  
3. **Reduce survey burden** through shorter, targeted, optional-comment formats.  
4. **Provide multiple routes**: anonymous forms, in-person discussion, representative channels, embedded digital tools and direct staff contact.  
5. **Clarify feedback pathways and accountability**, including who reads feedback and who can act.  
6. **Protect psychological safety**, especially for sensitive or staff-related feedback.  
7. **Include underrepresented and less engaged students**, rather than relying only on those already present and vocal.  
8. **Treat feedback as holistic**, connecting academic experience with wellbeing, resources, belonging and practical constraints.

Overall, students are not resistant to feedback; they are resistant to feedback systems that feel repetitive, opaque, unsafe, or performative. Good feedback culture is built when students can see that their voices lead to understanding, care, and meaningful change.
