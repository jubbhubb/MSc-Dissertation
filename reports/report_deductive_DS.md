# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good Feedback from the University

## Introduction

This report presents a thematic analysis of student accounts of feedback within the University. The data indicate that students understand “good feedback” broadly: not only as comments on academic work, but as a wider institutional feedback cycle in which students raise concerns, the University responds, and visible improvements follow. Across the dataset, effective feedback was described as accessible, relevant, timely, transparent, actionable, consistent and ongoing. Conversely, poor feedback was experienced as burdensome, generic, opaque, delayed, defensive, or disconnected from change.

Seven overarching themes are discussed below.

---

## 1. User-friendly feedback

Students repeatedly emphasised that feedback systems must be easy to access and low effort to complete. Long forms, repeated surveys and mandatory open-text questions were viewed as major barriers. One student asked, “why do I have to fill like nine different questions when I only want to give one point across?” Others described being “bombarded with surveys” or giving up when forms took longer than advertised.

Students preferred shorter, structured formats such as Likert scales, sliders, yes/no questions or ratings, with optional comment boxes for elaboration. This was seen as allowing quick participation while still enabling richer feedback when students had more to say. A typical suggestion was: “how do you feel on a scale of one to 10… tick, tick, tick and add optional comments.”

Accessibility was also central. Students wanted feedback forms to work on smartphones, be compatible with assistive technologies, and avoid relying solely on email, which was often lost in inbox overload. Some students suggested QR codes, paper forms in class, embedded forms on course pages, tutorial discussions, suggestion boxes and face-to-face routes. However, students also noted that in-person feedback may exclude commuters or those unable to attend, meaning multimodal routes are necessary.

Psychological safety was a key part of user-friendliness. Many students were reluctant to give honest criticism if they feared identification, judgement, damaged relationships, or repercussions from staff with authority over them. Anonymity was therefore described as essential for candour: “if it’s not anonymous… I won’t do it.” At the same time, some students wanted identifiable routes when they needed a personal response. Good feedback systems therefore require choice: anonymous, confidential, mediated and direct options.

Students also valued human, respectful engagement. They were more willing to provide feedback when staff appeared genuinely interested, created relaxed spaces, and explicitly invited student voice. Dismissive responses undermined participation, while supportive dialogue made feedback feel worthwhile.

---

## 2. Goal-referenced feedback

Students wanted feedback to be clearly connected to specific courses, problems, learning goals and assessment expectations. Generic feedback forms were strongly criticised. Students felt that standardised questions such as “what could we do better?” were too broad and did not help them focus on concrete issues. They preferred tailored prompts about particular lectures, topics, assessment tasks, workloads or course structures.

Course-specific relevance was particularly important. Students described identical forms across schools as disconnected from the actual learning experience. They wanted questions “tailored to the course… based on what they’ve seen.” Feedback was seen as more useful when staff asked about observed problems, such as low lecture attendance or widespread difficulty with coursework, rather than relying only on general satisfaction measures.

Students also wanted feedback processes to clarify their purpose. They were more motivated when the University explained why feedback was being collected, who would use it, and what it was intended to improve. Without this, surveys felt like routine administration rather than meaningful consultation.

Goal-referenced feedback also included alignment between stated expectations and lived experience. Students raised concerns about workload, course pacing, credit value, assessment preparation and exam design. For example, some felt that exams did not reflect taught content or that sample papers did not match exam difficulty. Good feedback, in this sense, should help the University assess whether teaching, workload and assessment are aligned with learning outcomes and professional expectations.

---

## 3. Consistent feedback

Students reported substantial inconsistency between courses, schools and staff. Some courses were described as well organised, responsive and transparent, while others provided little follow-up. This variation undermined confidence in the University-wide feedback system: “it really varies on how much they then fed back to us.”

Students wanted shared standards of good practice. Positive examples included courses where staff summarised survey findings, explained what would change, and implemented short-term adjustments. Students suggested that less effective courses could learn from better-organised ones, for example in how Learn pages are structured or how feedback outcomes are communicated.

Consistency also related to fairness and reliability in assessment feedback. Students raised concerns about variable tutor quality, unclear peer review systems, inconsistent marking, and lack of moderation. Where feedback or grades appeared dependent on the tutor, marker or course, students perceived the process as unfair.

A further consistency issue concerned institutional signposting. Students described being passed between departments, receiving conflicting advice, or being unsure whether an issue belonged to complaints, appeals, course teams or central services. This created frustration and delayed resolution. Good feedback requires reliable pathways, joined-up communication and clear ownership.

Students also identified potential conflicts of interest when critical feedback goes only to the person responsible for the course. Some suggested external review, independent oversight or an ombudsman-type role to ensure feedback is interpreted objectively and acted upon consistently.

---

## 4. Timely feedback

Timing was one of the strongest themes. Students consistently argued that feedback is most valuable when it can benefit the current cohort. End-of-semester or post-exam feedback was often viewed as too late: “that won’t help us to solve the ongoing problem with the course.” This was especially important for short courses and one-year programmes, where delayed change may only benefit future students.

Mid-semester feedback was valued when it led to change while the course was still running. Students gave positive examples of staff adjusting teaching delivery, uploading materials earlier, arranging Q&A sessions, or extending deadlines in response to immediate concerns. One example involved a quick poll followed by a deadline extension within hours, which students saw as highly effective.

However, students also noted that mid-semester feedback can be too early if they have not yet experienced enough of the course, or too late if responses are delayed until the final weeks. This suggests timing should be matched to course length and assessment points. Several students proposed earlier check-ins around weeks two to four, pre-assessment feedback opportunities, and mechanisms for raising issues as they occur.

Timeliness also applied to support services and administrative feedback. Delayed replies, unclear escalation and being passed between contacts reduced usefulness. One student noted that by the time a response arrived, they had already resolved the issue themselves. Good feedback therefore requires prompt acknowledgement, response tracking and clear escalation timelines.

---

## 5. Tangible and transparent feedback

Students repeatedly described feedback as disappearing into a “void” when they did not know who received it, whether it was read, or what happened next. Transparency was therefore a core condition of good feedback.

Students wanted to know where feedback goes, who reads it, who has responsibility for acting on it, and how decisions are made. Anonymous automated forms were seen as particularly opaque: “I don’t know who received the feedback, I don’t know if someone read the feedback.” In contrast, face-to-face feedback, course emails, response documents and feedback tables made the process feel more visible.

Closing the feedback loop was the dominant subtheme. Students wanted acknowledgement, summaries of key issues, planned actions, reasons for non-action, and updates on progress. They did not always expect immediate change, but they wanted evidence of consideration: “tell us why” if the answer is no. Vague phrases such as “we will take this into account” were experienced as frustrating because they did not explain what would happen.

Transparency was also linked to trust and belonging. When students saw collective results, peer agreement, meeting minutes or response tables, they felt part of a shared improvement process. Seeing “how many of your peers agreed with you” was thought to motivate engagement and demonstrate the scale of issues. Conversely, repeated surveys without outcomes felt performative.

Students also wanted transparency about constraints, especially where issues involved external bodies or complex institutional structures. Even if the University could not fully resolve a problem, students wanted to know what had been attempted and what barriers remained.

---

## 6. Actionable feedback

Students defined effective feedback by whether it leads to improvement. “Take action, please” summarised a recurring expectation. Feedback that is merely collected, acknowledged vaguely, or discussed without practical consequences was not considered effective.

Actionable feedback involved specific issue-to-action mapping. Students valued responses that stated: this is the issue, this is our solution; this will change now; this will change later; this cannot change and here is why. Positive examples included deadline extensions, additional live teaching sessions, earlier release of materials, Q&A sessions and changes to course content or format.

Students also wanted feedback to reach people with power to act. Course organisers were often seen as best placed for course-level issues because they understand the context, but students wanted escalation when local staff could not or would not respond. Feedback routes without authority were perceived as ineffective.

Academic assessment feedback was also judged by actionability. Marks alone, brief comments, or generic statements such as “good” or “bad” were insufficient. Students wanted explanations of mistakes, reasons for marks, and guidance for improvement: “to know why you made a mistake, what was good… so that you can move forward.” Rich feedback from tutors encouraged students to provide better feedback in return, suggesting a reciprocal culture of feedback quality.

Actionability also required receptiveness. Defensive or dismissive responses to criticism discouraged future engagement. Students preferred solution-focused responses, including compromise where exact requests were not possible.

---

## 7. Ongoing feedback

Students did not want feedback to be a one-off end-point. They described good feedback as a continuous cycle of listening, responding, implementing and checking back. Regular short-cycle feedback was preferred over isolated long surveys. Suggestions included weekly or fortnightly check-ins, monthly course-rep meetings, always-open course feedback forms, tutorial feedback moments, and structured feedback points across the semester.

Ongoing feedback was important for sustaining motivation. When students saw previous feedback acted on, they became more willing to participate: “seeing feedback being brought to action is definitely encouraging.” Conversely, repeated unresolved issues damaged credibility. Students were particularly frustrated when the same problems were raised year after year with no visible change.

Students recognised that some changes take time and may benefit future cohorts. However, they objected to systems where feedback only ever helps the next year’s students. Good feedback should include both short-term actions for current students and longer-term improvements for future cohorts.

Student representatives were seen as valuable ongoing intermediaries, especially for sensitive or collective issues, but only if they had clear destinations for feedback and could report back on outcomes. Without return communication, representative systems felt incomplete.

---

## Conclusion

Students described good University feedback as a reciprocal, visible and improvement-oriented process. It should be easy to give, clearly linked to relevant goals, consistent across contexts, timely enough to matter, transparent in its route and outcomes, actionable in practice, and sustained over time.

The strongest message across the analysis is that collecting feedback is not enough. Students need to see that their input is received, understood, discussed by the right people, and translated into clear action or explanation. Where this happens, feedback builds trust, engagement and shared responsibility. Where it does not, students experience feedback as burdensome, performative and demotivating.

Key priorities for the University are therefore: reduce survey burden; offer multiple accessible and anonymous routes; tailor questions to course-specific issues; create clear ownership and escalation pathways; respond earlier within courses; publish summaries, decisions and action plans; and establish ongoing feedback cycles that benefit both current and future students.
