# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Feedback Effective for Students

## Overview

This thematic analysis identifies what students perceive as “good feedback” within the University context. Across the data, students did not define good feedback simply as the opportunity to complete a survey. Rather, effective feedback was understood as a relational, safe, transparent, and responsive process in which student concerns are heard by people with authority, acted on where possible, and communicated back clearly. A strong latent theme was that feedback is closely tied to institutional care: when feedback disappears, students feel ignored; when it is acknowledged and acted on, students feel valued and more willing to engage.

The findings are organised below by the overarching themes identified in the dataset.

---

## 1. Timely, Actionable, and Visible Institutional Response

The strongest and most recurrent theme was that feedback must lead to visible response. Students repeatedly described current systems as “screaming into a void” or “talking to a wall” when no acknowledgement or outcome was communicated. Good feedback was defined as feedback that produces change, or at minimum, a clear explanation of why change is not possible.

Students particularly valued rapid, observable action within the same semester: “three hours later… we decided to extend the deadline” made one student feel “my voices will be heard.” Conversely, end-of-course surveys were often seen as too late, especially for one-year programmes or short courses: “what about my cohort?” Students recognised that some changes benefit future cohorts, but felt this was insufficient when current students had already experienced harm.

Closing the feedback loop was therefore central. Students wanted summaries of feedback received, action plans, timelines, and updates on progress. Vague statements such as “we will look into it” or “we will take this into account” were experienced as frustrating unless linked to specific actions. Even partial or compromise responses were valued where they showed effort.

---

## 2. Quality and Design of Feedback Mechanisms

Students wanted feedback tools that are short, relevant, specific, and expressive. Generic, copy-pasted surveys were criticised for failing to capture course-specific or lived experience concerns. Students preferred structured formats such as rating scales for efficiency, but only when paired with optional open-text boxes so they could explain the “why” behind dissatisfaction.

A key tension emerged between brevity and depth. Long open-text surveys deter completion, yet closed questions alone restrict expression. Good survey design therefore requires concise rating items, optional qualitative detail, progress indicators, save-progress functions, and questions tailored to the course or issue. Students also wanted constructive prompts asking what worked well, what did not, why, and how improvement might happen.

Students were critical of feedback requests that seemed to ask about superficial priorities rather than substantive issues. Feedback should be purposeful: students wanted to know why they were being asked, what the University intended to do with the data, and how the questions related to possible change.

---

## 3. Clear Communication, Navigation, and Process Usability

Many students did not know where feedback goes, who reads it, or which route to use for different issues. This lack of navigability undermined trust and increased frustration. Students described being passed between offices, receiving contradictory information, or escalating simple issues to senior staff because no clear route existed.

Good feedback systems require clear pathways: who to contact, what types of feedback belong where, how issues are escalated, and who is accountable for responding. Students suggested a central feedback/help hub or ombuds-style function to route issues, prevent deflection, and track progress.

Communication channels also mattered. Email was frequently described as overloaded and ineffective. Students recommended embedding feedback links in course pages, using QR codes, dedicated representative spaces on Learn, and multiple modes including online, paper, in-class, and conversational routes. However, QR codes and online forms must be mobile-friendly, easy to return to, and not lost if a student closes a browser tab.

---

## 4. Safety, Privacy, and Protection

Psychological safety was essential to honest feedback. Students feared being identified, judged, or treated differently, particularly when giving criticism about lecturers, tutors, marking, accommodation, or discriminatory experiences. Even where feedback was said to be anonymous, students worried that unique circumstances, names, or small-group contexts could make them identifiable.

Anonymity was therefore viewed as crucial for accuracy and participation: “if it’s not [anonymous], it’s really not going to get… accurate” responses. However, students also recognised that named feedback can be useful when they want a direct response. The preferred model was choice: anonymous, confidential, and named routes depending on sensitivity and desired follow-up.

Residential complaints raised additional protection issues. In shared accommodation, even anonymised complaints may reveal the complainant, creating risk when students must continue living with the person complained about. Students wanted systems that protect complainants after feedback, not merely collect the complaint.

---

## 5. Trust, Accountability, and Institutional Power

Students often imagined the University as large, faceless, hierarchical, and difficult to influence. Feedback was perceived to get “lost in the system,” with responsibility deflected between departments. This damaged trust, especially when repeated concerns persisted across cohorts.

Good feedback requires accountability beyond individual goodwill. Students wanted feedback to reach people with power to act, with escalation where local course organisers do not respond. They also identified potential conflicts of interest when course staff alone receive feedback about their own teaching, suggesting independent review or external moderation.

Students distinguished individual staff care from institutional failure. Many believed staff cared personally, but systems prevented effective response. Trust increased when named senior staff responded, when action items were linked to issues, and when students could see that feedback had been reviewed beyond the immediate course team.

---

## 6. Engagement, Motivation, and Feedback Burden

Students’ willingness to give feedback depended on perceived usefulness, effort, timing, relevance, and incentives. Survey fatigue was widespread: students felt “bombarded” by repeated requests, especially when previous feedback had not produced visible change. This led to superficial “click-through” behaviour or disengagement.

Students were motivated by a desire for change, improvement for future cohorts, financial investment in their education, and feeling heard. Incentives such as vouchers, chocolate, extra credit, or access to useful materials could increase participation, but were not substitutes for meaningful action.

Feedback was experienced as labour. Long forms, emotional effort, tactful wording, and chasing responses all consumed time. Good systems should reduce unnecessary burden through shorter forms, embedded completion time, optional comments, and avoiding busy assessment periods.

---

## 7. Accessible and Inclusive Participation

Students emphasised that one feedback mode cannot reach everyone. In-person feedback can exclude commuters, disabled students, absent students, distant-campus students, and those with work or caring responsibilities. Email-only or digital-only routes can exclude students who are overwhelmed, do not check emails, or find written English difficult.

Inclusive feedback should be multimodal and accessible by default. Suggested approaches included recorded lecture QR codes, course-page feedback forms, hybrid options, paper forms, office hours, open forums, targeted listening groups, and proactive outreach to underrepresented communities.

Distant-campus students, particularly vet students, described feeling excluded from central services and University identity. Transport, campus access, and service availability were not peripheral issues; they shaped whether students could participate in feedback and university life at all.

---

## 8. Dialogic, Relational, and Valuing Feedback

Students valued feedback as dialogue rather than one-way data extraction. Focus groups, tutorials, small-class discussions, and face-to-face conversations were seen as richer because students could explain, hear others, develop ideas, and receive immediate acknowledgement.

Relational trust mattered. Students were more willing to speak when staff explicitly invited feedback, appeared open, and responded non-defensively. Dismissive, hostile, or defensive staff responses were highly damaging and could overshadow otherwise positive experiences. A single experience of being laughed at or dismissed made feedback feel unsafe and pointless.

Good feedback also communicates respect. Students described effective feedback as showing that “the school values our input.” Being asked can make students feel cared for, but only if the process is not performative.

---

## 9. Teaching, Assessment, Curriculum, and Learning Improvement

Much feedback concerned teaching quality, assessment design, workload, resources, and course organisation. Students wanted feedback systems to address workload-credit mismatch, clustered deadlines, unclear coursework instructions, inconsistent resources, poor assessment feedback, and large impersonal classes.

Students distinguished between the value of a course and problems in delivery: a course may be necessary, but taught or assessed poorly. They also challenged the assumption that subject expertise equals teaching quality. Feedback should therefore be used to improve pedagogy, not merely evaluate satisfaction.

Assessment feedback itself was a concern. Students criticised marks-only exam feedback and generic comments that do not support learning. Good academic feedback should be specific, explanatory, and actionable.

---

## 10. Student Support, Welfare, and Personal Circumstances

Students felt feedback forms often focused narrowly on academic delivery while neglecting workload, wellbeing, childcare, finance, housing, commuting, disability, and caring responsibilities. They argued that these issues directly affect learning and grades: “you can’t be saying… why are the grades going low, and not realizing it relates with how people are coping.”

Students wanted more holistic feedback systems that ask about wellbeing, campus experience, workload, and practical barriers. They also wanted solution-oriented responses, not simply statements that nothing can be done. Personal tutors, proactive check-ins, and personalised support were valued when they helped students disclose difficulties and access help.

---

## 11. Representation, Collective Voice, and Peer Mediation

Students worried that feedback often represents the most vocal, engaged, or available students, while quieter, overwhelmed, absent, marginalised, or low-engagement students are missed. Low response rates reduced confidence in the representativeness of feedback.

Class representatives were valued as intermediaries, but their role was unevenly understood and sometimes overburdened. Reps could feel dismissed, blamed, or powerless. Students wanted better rep visibility, clearer channels, and institutional responsibility for action rather than relying on unpaid student labour.

Peer-visible or collectively endorsed feedback was seen as empowering because it shows whether concerns are widespread. Group settings also reduce isolation by helping students realise others share the same issue.

---

## 12. Community, Belonging, and Institutional Culture

Finally, feedback was shaped by whether students felt part of a community. Where students experienced the University as transactional, distant, or fragmented, motivation to give feedback decreased. Belonging encouraged students to contribute not only for themselves but for peers and future cohorts.

Students contrasted societies and small classes, which felt relational and responsive, with larger institutional structures, which felt impersonal. Distant-campus students described a particularly strong lack of connection to the wider University. Building a feedback culture therefore requires more than better surveys; it requires visible relationships, community, and institutional presence.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Close the feedback loop consistently**: publish “you said, we did/we cannot because” summaries with timelines and follow-up updates.  
2. **Collect feedback early enough to benefit current students**, especially in short courses and one-year programmes.  
3. **Create clear routes and accountability**, including a central hub or oversight function for routing and tracking issues.  
4. **Use multimodal feedback systems**: anonymous surveys, optional named routes, tutorials, focus groups, paper forms, QR codes, and course-page links.  
5. **Design concise but expressive surveys** with ratings, optional comments, progress bars, save functions, and course-specific questions.  
6. **Protect anonymity and psychological safety**, particularly for sensitive staff, marking, accommodation, disability, and discrimination concerns.  
7. **Broaden feedback beyond course delivery** to include wellbeing, workload, facilities, transport, finance, housing, and campus belonging.  
8. **Strengthen representation** by supporting class reps, reaching quieter groups, and targeting underrepresented students through trusted channels.  
9. **Train staff in non-defensive feedback response**, acknowledgement, and transparent explanation.  
10. **Ensure visible action from feedback**, even if partial, because evidence of action is the strongest driver of future engagement.

In summary, students view good feedback as a reciprocal process: safe to give, easy to access, meaningful to complete, transparently handled, and visibly acted upon.
