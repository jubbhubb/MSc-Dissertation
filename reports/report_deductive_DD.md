# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good University Feedback?

## Introduction

This report presents a thematic analysis of student accounts of feedback processes at the University. The data suggest that students understand “good feedback” in two linked ways: feedback they receive on their learning and assessment, and feedback they give to the University about courses, services, and student experience. Across both meanings, students consistently judged feedback by whether it was specific, timely, usable, transparent, and capable of producing change. A recurring concern was that students are often asked for feedback but do not see what happens afterwards, creating a sense that feedback “goes into a void”.

Seven overarching themes were identified: Goal-referenced, User-friendly, Timely, Tangible and transparent, Actionable, Ongoing, and Consistent. A further set of cross-cutting issues concerned psychological safety, institutional responsiveness, and representation.

---

## 1. Goal-referenced feedback: clear purpose, relevance, and alignment

Students valued feedback processes when they were clearly connected to a specific course, assessment, lecture, issue, or learning goal. Broad or generic feedback requests were experienced as difficult to answer and less meaningful. Students described generic forms as “copy-pasted” across courses, with “the only thing that changes” being staff names. In contrast, specific prompts such as “how do you feel about lecture three?” or questions about a particular topic helped students provide more focused and useful responses.

A key feature of good feedback was clarity about why feedback was being requested. Students wanted the University to explain “why they want feedback on this particular thing and what they want to do with it”. Without this, surveys could feel like administrative routines rather than tools for improvement.

Students also wanted feedback opportunities to address the issues that mattered to them, rather than only those pre-defined by the University. Some felt forms asked about “the problems that the university thinks that we have” rather than the problems students actually experience. This was particularly evident around workload, wellbeing, facilities, transport, accessibility, and assessment design.

In relation to academic feedback, students wanted alignment between teaching, assessment, and professional or learning goals. They criticised exams or sample papers that did not reflect what had been taught, and workload that felt inconsistent with credit value. Good feedback, therefore, is not only about asking students what they think, but about situating those questions within clear educational aims and realistic student experiences.

---

## 2. User-friendly feedback: low burden, accessible, safe, and inclusive

Students repeatedly identified burden as a major barrier to engagement. Long surveys, repeated forms, mandatory open-text boxes, and inaccurate time estimates discouraged participation. Students reported “survey fatigue” and described feedback forms as “tedious”, “boring”, and “too long”. Several said they stopped completing surveys when they felt overwhelmed or when forms took longer than advertised.

Preferred formats were short, simple, and flexible: Likert scales, sliders, yes/no questions, progress bars, optional comment boxes, paper slips, QR codes, post-it notes, and brief in-class activities. Students liked being able to “tick” or “choose” quickly, while still having the option to explain if they had more to say. However, they also warned that closed questions alone can restrict meaningful feedback; effective design should combine low effort with space for elaboration.

Accessibility was another central issue. Students reported that some forms were not smartphone-friendly, difficult to find, or lost in crowded email inboxes. Email-only processes were described as excluding some students, including those with ADHD, commuters, international students, and students managing heavy workloads. Students suggested embedding feedback links directly in course pages, offering multiple routes, and ensuring compatibility with assistive technologies.

Psychological safety was essential. Many students feared being identifiable, judged, or treated differently if they gave critical feedback. Anonymity was described as necessary for honest participation: “I won’t do it if not anonymous.” Students wanted choice: anonymous forms for sensitive issues, identifiable routes where follow-up was useful, and mediated routes through representatives for concerns about specific staff.

Students also valued human and relational feedback spaces. Tutorials, office hours, focus groups, listening groups, and informal conversations were often seen as more meaningful than impersonal online forms, provided they were safe and low-pressure.

---

## 3. Timely feedback: early enough to matter

Timing was one of the strongest themes. Students consistently argued that feedback is most useful when it can benefit the current cohort. End-of-semester or post-exam feedback was often seen as “too late” because it could not solve ongoing problems. One student asked: “What about my cohort?” Another said feedback at the end of a short course had little point because they wanted changes “in between the course so that it gets better for the rest of the 2, 3 weeks”.

Mid-semester feedback was valued when it led to actual mid-semester change. Positive examples included deadline extensions, earlier release of materials, additional Q&A sessions, more live teaching, and adjusted revision topics. Students were particularly impressed when responses happened within days or even hours.

However, students also noted that midterm feedback can be poorly timed if it clashes with assessment deadlines or occurs before they have enough experience to comment. Good timing therefore requires balance: feedback should be early enough to support change, but late enough for students to have informed views. Some suggested weeks two to four, regular short check-ins, or always-open feedback channels.

Timeliness also applied to support and escalation. Students described frustration when emails were passed between departments, when promised escalation took weeks, or when support arrived after they had already solved the issue themselves. In these cases, delay made feedback ineffective.

---

## 4. Tangible and transparent feedback: visible processes, recipients, and outcomes

Students wanted to know where feedback goes, who reads it, and what happens next. A common concern was that online forms were anonymous “to who knows”; students did not know whether anyone read them or whether they reached decision-makers. This lack of transparency reduced trust and willingness to participate.

Closing the feedback loop was central. Students valued acknowledgement that feedback had been received, summaries of what students said, and clear explanations of planned action. Examples of good practice included course-wide emails, feedback tables, meeting minutes, and staff spending class time explaining survey results. Students appreciated statements such as “this is what people said” and “this is what we’re going to do”.

Transparency did not always require immediate change. Students recognised that some requests may be impossible or slow. However, they wanted reasons: “Sometimes we ask, and the answer is no, but tell us why.” Vague phrases such as “we will take this into account” were experienced as frustrating because they did not explain what would happen, when, or why not.

Students also wanted transparency about constraints, especially where issues involved external partners or multiple departments. For example, transport concerns required the University to explain what negotiations had taken place and what progress had been made. Transparency was seen as a way to build trust, community, and empathy for staff roles and limitations.

---

## 5. Actionable feedback: leading to practical change

Students defined effective feedback by whether it produced action. They repeatedly contrasted being “heard” with being acted upon. Acknowledgement alone was not enough if it was followed by “nothing”. Good feedback should identify concrete next steps, practical solutions, or at least a plan for further discussion.

Students valued responses that mapped issues to solutions: “this is the issue, and this is our solution.” Positive examples included extending deadlines, changing upload schedules, adding live sessions, revisiting difficult topics, organising Q&As, and improving course materials. Even partial solutions were valued when exact requests could not be met.

Actionable feedback also required specificity and depth. In assessment feedback, students criticised comments that were only numbers, grades, or brief statements such as “good” or “bad”. Useful feedback explained why marks were awarded, what was wrong, what was strong, and how to improve. One positive example involved a lecturer writing comments for each question explaining “why you got this point”.

Students also wanted feedback to reach people empowered to act: course organisers, programme directors, heads of school, service leads, or external accountability roles where needed. Feedback channels were seen as ineffective when the recipient lacked authority or when responsibility was passed between departments.

---

## 6. Ongoing feedback: continuous cycles rather than one-off surveys

Students preferred feedback as a continuing relationship rather than an isolated survey. They described effective feedback as a cycle: gather feedback, summarise it, respond, implement change, and check back later. Without follow-up, motivation declined: students became “less willing” to give feedback when they never saw outcomes.

Continuous mechanisms included weekly or fortnightly check-ins, monthly course-rep meetings, always-open forms, tutorial feedback cycles, group chats feeding into representatives, office hours, and scheduled individual check-ins. Students particularly valued embedded practices where tutors regularly asked for feedback and reported back on previous comments.

Ongoing feedback also included improvement across cohorts. Students accepted that some changes may benefit future students, but were discouraged when every cohort raised the same issues and “nothing changed”. Repeated unresolved problems damaged credibility. Conversely, seeing previous cohort feedback incorporated into course design strengthened confidence in the system.

The data suggest that feedback should operate at multiple rhythms: rapid channels for urgent issues, regular in-course opportunities for adjustment, and larger reviews for structural improvement.

---

## 7. Consistent feedback: reliable systems, standards, and fairness

Students reported considerable variation between courses, schools, and services. Some courses had responsive staff, clear summaries, and action plans; others provided no visible response. This inconsistency made good feedback dependent on individual goodwill rather than institutional standards.

Students wanted more standardised feedback infrastructure: a consistent platform, clear routes, shared expectations, and predictable follow-up. They also wanted reliable institutional guidance. Conflicting advice between departments, complaints, and appeals undermined trust and left students asking, “What am I supposed to do?”

Consistency also related to fairness in assessment and teaching support. Students raised concerns about variation in tutor quality, unclear peer moderation, unfair group-work weighting, and inconsistent marking or moderation. They wanted clearer criteria, reliable moderation, and independent review where feedback concerned the staff member responsible for the course. External or higher-level oversight was suggested to reduce defensiveness and conflicts of interest.

---

## Cross-cutting issues: safety, representation, and institutional responsiveness

Several important issues cut across all themes. Students questioned whether a large university system could be responsive to individual voices. Some felt departments “push the question around” rather than take ownership. Others worried that feedback systems overrepresent engaged, vocal, or attending students while missing those who are struggling, absent, quiet, disabled, international, commuting, or under pressure.

Students also emphasised that feedback should not be limited narrowly to academic delivery. Workload, wellbeing, facilities, accessibility, transport, accommodation, childcare, and financial pressures shape learning and should be legitimate topics for student feedback.

---

## Conclusion

For students, good feedback is not simply the collection of opinions. It is a visible, safe, timely, and purposeful process that leads to understanding, action, and continued dialogue. The strongest message is that students are willing to give feedback when they believe it matters. To sustain engagement, the University should reduce burden, tailor questions, provide multiple accessible and anonymous routes, act while current students can benefit, and routinely close the loop by showing what was heard, what will change, what cannot change, and why.
