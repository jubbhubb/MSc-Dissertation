# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good University Feedback Processes for Students

## Introduction

This thematic analysis examined student perspectives on what makes feedback to and from the University meaningful, trustworthy, and useful. Across the data, students did not view “good feedback” simply as the opportunity to complete surveys. Rather, good feedback was understood as a reciprocal institutional process: students provide views, the University acknowledges them, explains what will happen next, acts where possible, and communicates clearly where change is not possible. Six overarching themes were identified.

---

## 1. Responsiveness and Closing the Feedback Loop

The strongest theme was the need for the University to close the feedback loop. Students repeatedly described feedback as futile when they invested time in surveys, meetings, emails, or complaints and then heard nothing back: “you do... all the effort to put all your stuff down and send it and then nothing happens.” This absence of response reduced motivation to participate in future feedback exercises.

Students emphasised that acknowledgement alone can be valuable, especially where immediate change is not possible. They wanted to know that feedback had been received, read, and considered. Good feedback from the University therefore includes a clear response: what was raised, what will change, what cannot change, and why. One student summarised this directly: “sometimes the answer is no, but tell us why.”

Timeliness was also central. Students valued changes that benefited the current cohort, rather than only future students. This was particularly important in short courses, semester-long modules, and one-year programmes, where delayed changes could feel unfair: “I want change now.” However, students also recognised that large institutional changes take time. The issue was not always delay itself, but the lack of communication about progress.

Staff attitude shaped perceptions of responsiveness. Students distinguished staff who actively invited and engaged with feedback from those who appeared defensive, dismissive, or aggressive. Defensive responses discouraged future participation, whereas visible action built trust: “seeing feedback being brought to action is definitely an encouraging thing.”

---

## 2. Reciprocal Engagement and Motivation

Students’ willingness to provide feedback was shaped by whether they believed the process was worthwhile. Some students were motivated by altruism and a desire to improve the experience for future cohorts: “If I... can be able to make it easier for someone who's coming after me? Why not?” Others were more motivated when they could see direct personal or cohort benefit.

Survey fatigue was a major barrier. Students felt “bombarded” by repeated requests across courses and the wider University. Long, repetitive, or generic forms led to disengagement and poorer-quality responses. This was particularly true when students could not see how long a survey would take, whether progress was saved, or why the same questions were being asked repeatedly.

Incentives were seen as a practical way to increase participation, especially among students who might not otherwise attend feedback sessions or complete surveys. Vouchers, extra credit, or useful resources were mentioned as motivating. However, students also suggested that motivation should not rely only on rewards. Where students felt part of a community, they were more likely to contribute for collective benefit. Conversely, where their relationship with the University felt transactional, they were less likely to “bother.”

A reciprocal dynamic was also identified: students may give more thoughtful feedback when they themselves receive detailed, respectful academic feedback. The quality of staff feedback can model the quality of feedback students then provide in return.

---

## 3. Accessible and Inclusive Feedback Systems

Students wanted feedback systems that were easy to access, simple to complete, and varied enough to suit different needs. No single method was seen as sufficient. Participants recommended multiple avenues, including online forms, paper forms, in-class activities, QR codes, tutorials, student representatives, anonymous suggestion boxes, and in-person discussions.

Short and simple mechanisms were preferred for routine feedback. Students suggested brief polls, rating scales, smiley/sad buttons, or quick questions at the end of lectures. These methods were seen as less burdensome and more likely to capture immediate experiences. In-class or paper feedback was also valued because it allowed students to complete feedback while already present, although students recognised this may exclude those who are absent or disengaged.

A consistent, standardised feedback platform across courses was recommended. Students found multiple systems confusing and wanted clarity about where to submit feedback, whether it was anonymous, and who would read it. A continuous online suggestion box was also suggested so students could raise issues as they arise rather than waiting for formal survey periods.

Accessibility barriers were significant. Time poverty affected students with jobs, caring responsibilities, commuting demands, heavy workloads, or multiple deadlines. Commuter students and those based on geographically separate campuses faced additional barriers to attending in-person sessions and accessing services. Email was also criticised as an unreliable communication channel because feedback requests were often lost among large volumes of messages.

Inclusive access was particularly important for international students, students with disabilities, and students for whom English is an additional language. Long English-language emails and forms could be effortful, while email-based systems could be especially difficult for students with ADHD or other access needs. These findings suggest that good feedback systems must be designed inclusively from the outset, not added as an afterthought.

---

## 4. Constructive, Relevant, and Holistic Feedback Content

Students wanted feedback questions to be specific, relevant, and targeted. Generic survey templates were often experienced as unhelpful: “the surveys are very generic.” Students found it easier to respond when questions related directly to the course, week, assessment, workload, or issue being experienced.

Much student feedback concerned teaching quality, course organisation, assessment clarity, and workload. Students raised concerns about unclear instructions, poorly matched assessment expectations, and workload that did not feel aligned with course credit weighting: “The amount of work they're requesting... doesn't match 10 credits.” Students felt that formal workload calculations did not always reflect lived experience.

Participants also wanted feedback systems to move beyond purely academic delivery. They identified wellbeing, workload, isolation, pastoral support, transport, accommodation, finance, facilities, timetabling, and accessibility as legitimate areas for feedback. Some students felt that when they raised wellbeing concerns linked to course pressure, these were deflected to generic support services rather than recognised as connected to teaching and workload design. As one student noted, referring students elsewhere can feel like “not acknowledging that you might be the source of the problem.”

Good feedback systems should therefore invite constructive detail: what the issue is, why it matters, how widespread it may be, and what improvement could look like. Students distinguished between constructive feedback and “just complaints,” suggesting that well-designed prompts can help students provide more useful input.

---

## 5. Psychological Safety, Trust, and Student Voice

Psychological safety was fundamental. Students needed to feel able to be honest without fear of judgement, bias, or retaliation. Anonymity was therefore a major condition for candid feedback: “I want to be harsh sometimes... I can do that anonymously.” Students were particularly concerned where feedback related to lecturers, tutors, administrators, or others with power over grades, opportunities, or day-to-day treatment.

Partial anonymity was viewed as insufficient. For example, platforms that are anonymous to peers but identifiable to instructors did not feel safe. Students wanted clear information about who can see their identity and, ideally, flexibility to choose whether to remain anonymous or provide their name for follow-up.

In-person feedback was valued because it felt more human and responsive than submitting forms into a system. However, it also carried risks. Students were often uncomfortable criticising a staff member who was present in the room. Smaller tutorial groups or sessions led by someone other than the main lecturer were suggested as safer alternatives.

Student voice was also shaped by representation. Students recognised that feedback often overrepresents confident, engaged, vocal, or “keen” students, while underrepresenting those who are struggling, disengaged, absent, behind, commuting, or overloaded. This was seen as a serious limitation: “you’re kind of always asking the wrong people about what the issues are.” Large classes intensified this problem, making students feel less visible and less heard. Smaller classes were associated with stronger relationships, greater responsiveness, and more meaningful discussion.

Collective validation helped students feel safer. Discussing concerns with peers allowed students to realise they were not alone and gave them more confidence to raise issues. Public sharing of aggregate feedback could support this by showing whether concerns are shared across the cohort.

---

## 6. Institutional Accountability and Structural Constraints

Students often experienced the University as large, complex, and impersonal: a “faceless” institution or “wall” that absorbs feedback without dialogue. A recurring concern was that no one seemed clearly responsible for ensuring that feedback was answered, resolved, or escalated.

Students were often unsure who receives feedback, who has authority to act on it, or how to escalate concerns beyond the course level. This was especially problematic for non-academic issues such as finance, accommodation, disability support, transport, appeals, and facilities. Students described being passed between departments without resolution.

Student representatives were seen as important mediators who can collect, anonymise, and communicate collective feedback. However, the effectiveness of the rep system depended on visibility, training, communication channels, and whether staff acted on what representatives raised. Where issues did not appear in minutes or produce visible change, the system felt ineffective.

Students suggested that feedback should sometimes be reviewed by someone independent of the person or team being critiqued. External or neutral review was seen as a way to reduce conflicts of interest and strengthen accountability. This was especially relevant where feedback concerned staff conduct, repeated unresolved issues, or structural problems.

Students also recognised institutional constraints, including staff workload, strikes, resource pressures, and the difficulty of changing large systems quickly. However, recognising constraints did not remove the need for transparency. Students were more accepting of limits when the University explained them clearly and respectfully.

---

## Conclusion and Key Implications

For students, good feedback is not simply collected; it is acknowledged, acted upon, explained, and made visible. The University can strengthen student trust by designing feedback as a transparent relationship rather than a one-way data-gathering exercise.

Key implications are:

1. **Close the feedback loop** by reporting what was heard, what will change, what cannot change, and why.  
2. **Reduce survey burden** through shorter, targeted, and better-timed feedback requests.  
3. **Use multiple accessible channels**, including anonymous online options, in-class methods, tutorials, representatives, and continuous suggestion routes.  
4. **Ensure psychological safety** through genuine anonymity, clear identity settings, and neutral routes for sensitive concerns.  
5. **Make feedback more inclusive** by addressing barriers linked to disability, language, commuting, workload, employment, and disengagement.  
6. **Broaden feedback beyond courses** to include wellbeing, services, infrastructure, accommodation, transport, and student life.  
7. **Strengthen accountability** by clarifying who receives feedback, who is responsible for action, and how issues are escalated.  
8. **Share aggregate results and progress publicly** so students can see that their voice has impact.

Overall, students are willing to give meaningful feedback when they believe the University is listening. Trust is built through visible responsiveness, honest explanation, and systems that make all students—not only the most vocal—feel safe, included, and heard.
