# Thematic Analysis Report: What Makes Good University Feedback Processes

## Introduction

This report presents a thematic analysis of coded interview data exploring students’ views on what makes feedback to the University effective, meaningful, and worth engaging with. Across the dataset, students described feedback not simply as completing surveys, but as a relational and institutional process: they want to be asked in ways that are accessible, to feel safe and respected when speaking honestly, and to see evidence that their views lead to acknowledgement, explanation, or change.

Six overarching themes were identified:

1. Clarity, Transparency, and Accountability  
2. Accessible, Timely, and Well-Designed Feedback Processes  
3. Constructive and Actionable Learning Support  
4. Inclusive Student Voice and Engagement  
5. Relational, Trustworthy, and Emotionally Safe Feedback  
6. Consistency and Fairness Across the Student Experience  

Together, these themes suggest that “good feedback” is not defined only by the opportunity to submit comments, but by the full feedback cycle: invitation, accessibility, safety, response, action, and visible outcomes.

---

## 1. Clarity, Transparency, and Accountability

A dominant theme was that students often feel uncertain about where feedback goes, who reads it, and what authority the recipient has to act. Codes such as *unclear feedback routes*, *uncertainty about who reads feedback*, *feedback lost in institutional system*, and *being passed between services* suggest that feedback pathways are experienced as opaque and fragmented.

Students appear to distinguish between being asked for feedback and being meaningfully listened to. When feedback disappears without acknowledgement, students describe the process as performative, like “talking to a wall” or “screaming into a void”. This damages confidence in the institution and reduces future participation.

Accountability was especially important where issues cut across organisational boundaries, such as transport, accommodation, accessibility, assessment, or support services. Students perceived that responsibility could be deflected between departments, external providers, course organisers, and central University structures. This created a need for clearer ownership: students want to know who is responsible, what can realistically be changed, and where issues can be escalated if local routes fail.

Transparency was also linked to fairness. Students valued honest communication about constraints, including when change is not possible. However, they wanted this to be accompanied by explanation, evidence of consideration, and where possible alternative actions. A response that only justifies the status quo, without showing that student concerns have been understood, was perceived as dismissive.

**Good feedback therefore requires visible governance:** a clear route, named responsibility, documented responses, and communication about what has changed, what has not, and why.

---

## 2. Accessible, Timely, and Well-Designed Feedback Processes

Students described feedback design as central to participation. Over-reliance on emailed online surveys was frequently identified as problematic: surveys can be lost in inboxes, feel repetitive, be poorly suited to mobile devices, or arrive at times when students are already overloaded. Survey fatigue was a recurring barrier.

Good feedback processes were described as convenient, short, structured, and specific. Students often preferred rating scales for efficiency, but also wanted optional text boxes for specific or urgent issues. There was tension between highly structured forms, which reduce effort, and overly generic questions, which can feel disconnected from student-defined problems. Course-specific and student-designed questions were seen as more relevant and more likely to generate useful feedback.

Timing was one of the clearest findings. End-of-semester or end-of-course feedback was often experienced as too late to benefit the students providing it. Students wanted feedback opportunities early enough for current problems to be addressed, but not so early that they lacked sufficient experience to judge the course. This points to the value of staged or iterative feedback: brief early check-ins, mid-point review, and end-point evaluation, each with a clear purpose.

Students also favoured multiple modes: in-class feedback, paper forms, QR codes, embedded course-platform prompts, focus groups, tutorials, office hours, online surveys, and open continuous channels. No single method was viewed as sufficient. In-person feedback could feel more meaningful and acknowledged, but could reduce anonymity and exclude absent, commuting, shy, or disengaged students. Online methods increased flexibility but could feel impersonal or inaccessible.

**Good feedback therefore requires multimodal, low-friction design:** short, relevant, accessible, well-timed, and available through more than one route.

---

## 3. Constructive and Actionable Learning Support

Students’ motivation to give feedback was strongly linked to perceived impact. Repeated codes such as *feedback needs to lead to change*, *visible action*, *lack of evidence that feedback is implemented*, and *repeated feedback ignored* indicate that students judge the value of feedback by whether it produces tangible improvement.

Visible change did not always have to be large. Students valued partial actions, compromise solutions, rapid acknowledgement, and evidence of effort. Even where structural change was slow, they wanted to see that the University had understood the issue and was working towards a response. Conversely, repeated unresolved problems created futility and disengagement.

Students also framed feedback as part of learning support. They raised issues around teaching quality, assessment clarity, workload, accessibility support, extension decisions, and communication of academic expectations. Good feedback systems should therefore not be limited to satisfaction measurement; they should help identify barriers to learning and support timely intervention.

Students valued constructive prompts that helped them give useful, specific feedback rather than vague complaint. At the same time, they wanted the University to recognise that negative feedback often reflects unmet needs, stress, or barriers to participation. Feedback should be treated as diagnostic information, not simply criticism.

**Good feedback therefore requires actionability:** questions should invite specific, usable information, and institutional responses should identify concrete actions, timelines, or reasons for non-action.

---

## 4. Inclusive Student Voice and Engagement

Students were concerned that feedback systems often overrepresent the already engaged, confident, present, or vocal students. Codes highlighted low response rates, self-selecting feedback givers, absent students being missed, busy students being underrepresented, and disengaged students being overlooked.

Class and programme representatives were recognised as potentially valuable intermediaries, especially where they are visible, trusted, and able to communicate outcomes. However, the role of representatives was not always clear, and some students doubted whether reps had enough power or institutional access. Students also identified risks: intermediaries can feel unsafe if confidentiality is uncertain, and feedback may be filtered or softened before reaching decision-makers.

Informal peer spaces, including group chats and conversations, were common substitutes for formal feedback. These spaces can reveal shared concerns and reduce isolation, but may also escalate negativity if not connected to constructive channels. Students wanted the University to understand informal voice as a signal: when students complain to peers rather than use official systems, formal routes may feel inaccessible, futile, or unsafe.

Inclusion also meant recognising different student circumstances, including international students, low-income students, student parents, mature students, commuting students, disabled students, and those based on distant campuses. Students from less central or less connected communities described weaker belonging and poorer visibility within University structures.

**Good feedback therefore requires active inclusion:** the University should not wait only for confident students to speak, but should use varied, targeted, and community-based routes to reach quieter and underrepresented groups.

---

## 5. Relational, Trustworthy, and Emotionally Safe Feedback

Students’ willingness to provide honest feedback was deeply shaped by trust. Fear of negative consequences, fear of criticising staff directly, concern about grades, power hierarchies, and anxiety about identifiability were recurrent. Anonymity was therefore widely valued, particularly for sensitive or critical feedback.

However, anonymity was not the only need. Students also wanted relational feedback cultures in which staff actively invite feedback, respond non-defensively, and show care. Supportive personal tutors, proactive check-ins, small-group teaching, and face-to-face conversations were seen as routes that can make students feel valued. Feedback was more likely when students believed staff cared and were open to hearing difficult comments.

Dismissive or defensive responses were especially damaging. Students described negative experiences as overshadowing positive ones and reducing willingness to engage again. Even when staff were empathetic, students could lose trust if systems prevented resolution. This distinction is important: students may recognise individual care while still experiencing institutional failure.

Emotional safety also included the labour of giving feedback tactfully. Students may hesitate to speak if they worry about appearing rude, ungrateful, or personally critical. Feedback processes should therefore normalise constructive critique and protect students from retaliation or relational discomfort.

**Good feedback therefore requires psychological safety:** anonymity where needed, privacy for personal issues, non-defensive staff responses, and a culture that frames feedback as shared improvement rather than conflict.

---

## 6. Consistency and Fairness Across the Student Experience

Students wanted feedback systems to address the whole student experience, not only individual courses. Issues such as transport, accommodation, facilities, study space, timetabling, workload, assessment bunching, financial burden, and campus isolation were repeatedly identified as relevant to learning and wellbeing.

There was concern about inconsistency across courses, schools, platforms, support teams, and campuses. Some students experienced responsive staff and effective feedback loops, while others experienced delays, unclear communication, or no visible change. This variability created perceptions of unfairness.

Workload was a significant cross-cutting concern. Students reported course intensity, mismatch between credits and hours required, deadline bunching, and curriculum overload. Feedback mechanisms themselves could also become burdensome, consuming study time without clear benefit. When students are repeatedly asked for feedback but see no change, over-surveying can feel performative and disrespectful.

Students also recognised that feedback can be conflicting: different students want different things. Good systems therefore need to show how competing needs are balanced, rather than implying that all feedback can be implemented. Fairness requires transparent decision-making and communication about whose needs are being prioritised and why.

**Good feedback therefore requires institutional consistency:** common minimum standards for feedback routes, response times, accessibility, communication, and action reporting across the University.

---

## Overall Conclusions

The analysis indicates that good feedback is not simply a data collection exercise. Students define good feedback as a complete, trustworthy process in which:

- the purpose is clear;
- the route is accessible;
- the questions are relevant;
- participation feels safe;
- responses are timely;
- responsibility is identifiable;
- outcomes are visible;
- and students feel respected as partners in improvement.

The strongest message across the dataset is the need to close the feedback loop. Students are more willing to participate when they can see that feedback has been heard, considered, and acted upon. Conversely, lack of acknowledgement, unclear ownership, and repeated unresolved issues create fatigue, cynicism, and disengagement.

---

## Recommendations

1. **Create a transparent feedback map** showing where different types of feedback should go, who receives it, and expected response times.

2. **Introduce mandatory feedback loop closure**, including summaries of key issues raised, actions taken, actions not taken, and reasons why.

3. **Use multimodal feedback routes**, combining surveys with in-class opportunities, focus groups, representative systems, continuous channels, and targeted listening groups.

4. **Improve survey design** by using shorter, mobile-accessible, course-specific forms with structured questions and optional qualitative detail.

5. **Time feedback to benefit current students**, using early and mid-course check-ins as well as end-of-course evaluation.

6. **Strengthen accountability and escalation**, particularly for cross-departmental, accessibility, accommodation, transport, and support-service issues.

7. **Protect psychological safety**, including anonymity for sensitive feedback, clear confidentiality boundaries, and staff training in non-defensive responses.

8. **Increase representative visibility and power**, ensuring class and programme reps have clear roles, communication channels, and mechanisms to report outcomes back to students.

9. **Address whole-student experience issues**, not only teaching quality, recognising that workload, wellbeing, finances, transport, and belonging affect learning.

10. **Standardise minimum expectations across courses and services**, while allowing local flexibility for subject-specific needs.

In summary, students want feedback systems that are efficient but human, structured but flexible, anonymous when necessary but not faceless, and above all demonstrably connected to action.
